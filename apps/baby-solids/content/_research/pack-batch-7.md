## The grain-kernel rule — governs six foods here

> "Cook and finely grind or mash whole-grain kernels of wheat, barley, rice, and
> **other grains**." — CDC, `guideline`

Hazard list: "whole grain kernels of cooked barley, wheat, or other grains" ·
"**cooked or raw whole corn kernels**" · "crackers or breads with seeds, nut
pieces, or whole grain kernels."

**"Or other grains" does the work.** Quinoa, millet, buckwheat groats, teff and
rye berries aren't named but are cooked whole kernels and fall inside as
written. Grinding or mashing after cooking is issued; the *degree* is not.

**Corn is named twice** — "cooked or raw whole corn kernels" is independent, so
cooking does not discharge it. Only ground masa escapes.

## Toxicology

### 1. Inorganic arsenic in rice

FDA action level **100 ppb** for infant rice cereal — 2016 draft **finalized
2020-08-05, live, no retirement notice** (checked today).
<https://www.fda.gov/food/hfp-constituent-updates/fda-issues-final-guidance-industry-action-level-inorganic-arsenic-infant-rice-cereals>

> "Relative to body weight, rice intake for infants… is about **three times
> greater than for adults**… people consume the most rice (relative to their
> weight) at approximately **8 months of age**."

**The 8-month peak lands inside the 6–11 month band** — the load-bearing detail.

CDC: "**Only feeding your child infant rice cereal can increase their risk of
arsenic exposure**"; offer oat, barley, multigrain instead.

🚩 **The cooking trade-off is real and must be shown whole.** FDA: boiling rice
pasta-style in 6–10 parts water and draining "can reduce **40 to 60 percent** of
the inorganic arsenic" — but "also results in lowering the nutritional value…
reduces the levels of folate, **iron**, niacin and thiamine… by **50 to 70
percent**." Rinsing "has a minimal effect on the arsenic… and will wash off iron,
folate, thiamine and niacin." **For an iron-deficiency-risk infant these pull
opposite ways. Present both halves or neither.**

**Rice drinks:** NHS prohibits under 5 as a milk substitute ("may contain too
much arsenic"; page current, reviewed 2026-02-18). ⚠️ **No equivalent US
statement naming rice drinks was found** — cite NHS as NHS; don't imply a US
prohibition.

### 2. Cassava — cyanogenic glycosides

FAO: linamarin and lotaustralin; "cyanide content is substantially higher in the
cassava **peel**." Symptoms of raw or insufficiently processed cassava include
"vertigo, vomiting, collapse and in some cases death."

🚩 **The one actionable prep detail:** after 30 minutes' cooking, residual
cyanide is **~8% of initial when started in cold water** versus **~30% when
dropped into already-boiling water** — a ~4× difference. Soaking 18–24 h cuts
levels ~50%. Concentration "varies greatly between varieties."

Chronic exposure: konzo, irreversible spastic paraparesis, clustering in
children where water scarcity prevents adequate soaking. Notably "breastfed
children are not affected." (PMC13110498)

**Ruling:** peel completely, boil through from cold. Never raw, never lightly
cooked, never grated raw, never in a smoothie. ⚠️ **No verified ppm for US retail
yuca — publish none.**

### 3. Taro — calcium oxalate raphides

Hong Kong Centre for Food Safety (`reference` — a government risk body, not
pediatric guidance):

> "Damage to the plant cells during chewing… stimulates the forceful propulsion
> of the sharp raphides into the surrounding environment, stabbing the sensitive
> tissues of the tongue, gums and throat."

**The nuance usually got wrong:** "the acrid taste… is substantially reduced
after cooking, as cooking destroys the **enzymes**" — but "calcium oxalate
crystals cannot be dissolved in water and are **heat resistant**. Therefore,
washing and cooking cannot reliably remove calcium oxalates."

**So cooking denatures the enzymes that potentiate the sting; it does not remove
the needles.** Say that precisely. **Never wild taro** — "cooking cannot remove
the poisoning effect of wild taro."

⚠️ FAO's 780 mg/100 g oxalate figure is for "some species of cocoyam" — **not a
retail taro value; do not publish it as one.**

### 4. Potato — solanine

FAO: glycoalkaloids concentrate "in the flowers and sprouts (200 to 500 mg/100
g)"; healthy tubers usually <10 mg/100 g; **bitter varieties up to 80 mg/100 g
whole and 150–220 mg/100 g in the peel**. "**They are not destroyed during normal
cooking** because the decomposition temperature of solanine is about 243 °C."
Levels build with light exposure. They inhibit cholinesterase and cause
haemorrhagic GI and retinal damage.

**Taste is not a guard:** bitterness is undetectable "until… 20 mg/100 g" —
**twice the normal healthy-tuber level**, so mild elevation is imperceptible.

**Ruling: discard green or sprouted potatoes — do not merely trim.** Peel; the
peel is where they concentrate. ⚠️ No infant-specific threshold verified (EFSA
blocked).

### 5. Lotus root — the texture failure

**No guideline body names it.** The assessment is derived from a rule it plainly
fails: CDC's "cook food until it is soft enough to easily mash with a fork."

Lotus root's crispness is structural — aerenchyma plus lignified,
calcium-cross-linked cell walls hold texture through boiling, braising and
stewing. It is prized in Korean and Chinese cooking *precisely because* it stays
crunchy. **Potato, taro and cassava all pass the fork-mash test. Lotus root does
not, at any household cooking time.** Its culinary form is also a hard round
slice, and 연근조림 braises it in soy sauce and sugar — two more under-24-month
exclusions.

**Ruling (`expert_opinion`, explicitly derived — not `guideline`): not
appropriate before 12 months in any finger form.** Under 12m, only finely grated
or puréed into another food, contributing little. **If the canon includes it for
cultural completeness it should carry a "not a finger food" badge rather than a
modification instruction — there is no modification that makes a crisp root
mashable.** A human should ratify this call.

### 6. Buckwheat — the labeling asymmetry

Confirmed both directions (PMC12117482, 2025 review):
- **US:** not among the nine. Confirmed against FDA's own list.
- **Japan:** mandatory allergen labeling includes **buckwheat**; Japan also
  *prohibits* "may contain" statements by law and requires labeling above 10 ppm.
- **Korea:** requires labeling for **buckwheat**, pork, peach, tomato, chicken,
  beef beyond the Big Eight — "reflecting their prevalence as triggers of
  immediate-type food allergies among Korean children." Korea is "the only
  country where PAL is mandated by law."

🚩 **Guidance drift found: Japan's list is now 9, not 8** — cashew added
**2026-04-01** (Consumer Affairs Agency), after that review published; walnut
added 2023, macadamia added and matsutake removed 2024. **Cite "nine, as of April
2026" or avoid the count.**

**Why it matters here:** a Korean- or Japanese-market product carries a buckwheat
declaration the identical US product does not. A parent learning "buckwheat isn't
an allergen" from a US source will misread Korean labels; a parent learning it
from Korean relatives will look for a US label that legally need not exist.
**Both directions are wrong — state the asymmetry, not a verdict.**

Also: buckwheat is botanically gluten-free (a pseudocereal), **but soba is
commonly wheat-blended**, so "buckwheat is gluten-free" is false at product level.

### 7. Rye — gluten, not wheat

FDA: "Gluten describes a group of proteins found in certain grains (for example,
wheat, barley, and rye)." **Rye is not among the US 9 and is not wheat.** A
wheat-allergic child is not automatically rye-reactive; a rye product is not
automatically wheat-free — **most commercial rye bread contains wheat flour.**
Not a wheat-allergy substitute, and unsafe in celiac.

**ESPGHAN 2024 update** (doi 10.1002/jpn3.12280) — a reaffirming replacement of
2016, not a retirement:
> Gluten "at any time between completed 4 months (≥17 weeks) and 12 months…
> does not affect the cumulative incidence of CD."
> "**There is no specific guidance on the type of gluten-containing foods to be
> introduced at weaning.**"

That last sentence is the publishable one: **ESPGHAN explicitly declines to rank
gluten grains.**

### 8. Corn / nixtamalization — the brief was half wrong

**Calcium: confirmed, ~25×.** Corn grain Ca **7 mg** (`170288`) → corn tortilla
**175 mg** (`173241`). Real measurement, from the lime cook.

🚩 **Niacin: the claim does not survive the data.** USDA *total* niacin **falls**:
corn grain 3.63 mg → tortilla 1.5 mg per 100 g. What nixtamalization changes is
**bioavailability** of bound niacytin, per FAO ("A feature peculiar to niacin is
that it is bound and therefore not available… Some processing techniques
hydrolyze niacin, thereby making it available"). **The magnitude is unverified.**

**Correct phrasing:** nixtamalization sharply raises calcium and releases bound
niacin into an absorbable form; **it does not raise total niacin.** ⚠️ A human
must approve this wording — it contradicts the brief I gave.

🚩 **Fortified oat 64.1 vs cooked plain oatmeal 0.90 — a ~71× gap**, confirming
batch 1 independently. Fortified oat also carries **more than double** fortified
multigrain (64.1 vs 30.0): if the goal is iron, single-grain fortified oat is the
strongest non-rice option in USDA's data.

⚠️ **"Fortified" is not one thing.** Earth's Best organic rice cereal (`168975`)
is *"fortified only with iron"* — Fe 47.5 but **Zn 1.84**, a ~6× zinc shortfall
versus other fortified cereals. Read the panel.

### Injera — the allergen problem

🚩 Commercial injera is frequently **teff blended with wheat, barley or sorghum**
— a US-9 wheat exposure invisible to someone assuming "teff = gluten-free."
**Read the label; do not badge injera gluten-free.**
⚠️ **USDA has no injera record — zero hits. Every injera figure is unverified.**
Usually eaten with berbere-spiced stews (sodium, capsaicin) — serve plain.
⚠️ Some of teff's high iron is attributed in the literature to threshing-soil
contamination rather than the grain; **no quantified split verified — do not claim
teff iron is fully intrinsic.**

2. **The niacin bioavailability gain** from nixtamalization — directionally confirmed by FAO, magnitude unverified, and USDA total moves the other way.

## Disagreements to surface, not resolve

- **Rice drinks:** NHS prohibits under 5; no US body issues an equivalent named statement. Cite NHS as NHS.
- **Buckwheat:** mandatory-labeled in Japan and Korea, not an allergen in US law. Neither is "right" — **the asymmetry is the fact**, and it's load-bearing for a family reading both label systems.
- **Arsenic mitigation vs iron:** FDA's boil-and-drain removes 40–60% of arsenic *and* 50–70% of the added iron. FDA states both and reconciles neither. Neither should we.
- **Gluten grain choice:** ESPGHAN states outright there is no guidance on which gluten grain to introduce. Any ranking is editorial.
