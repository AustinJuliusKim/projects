## 🚨 Correction to the brief: there is no infant iodine UL

I asked for "any upper intake level for infants (NIH ODS has a UL)." **It does
not.** NIH ODS lists the UL for both 0–6 and 7–12 months as **"Not possible to
establish"** — the FNB judged the data insufficient. The footnote reads
"**Formula and food should be the only sources of iodine for infants.**"

AI is 110 mcg (0–6m) and 130 mcg (7–12m); the nearest real ceiling is the
**200 mcg UL at 1–3 years**, and applying that to an 11-month-old is inference,
not guideline. **This is an absence of a limit, not permission — and not a
reversal.** Label it if the app uses it.

## The mollusc/crustacean split — verified three ways

FDA's major-allergen lists (live, "content current as of 05/19/2023") give nine:
milk, egg, fish, **crustacean shellfish**, tree nuts, wheat, peanuts, soybeans,
sesame — glossed as "Crustacean shellfish (e.g., crab, lobster, shrimp)."
**Oyster, mussel, clam and scallop appear on none of them.**

FDA formalised the category in a **January 2025 final guidance** defining a
"non-listed food allergen" as "a food allergen other than one of the major food
allergens… listed in the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act", with a
citizen-petition route for adding one.

⚠️ FDA's Q&A is widely quoted as saying molluscs "are not" major allergens in so
many words. **That sentence could not be retrieved from a live page** (PDF-only,
no extraction available). The *exclusion* is verified by absence from three live
lists; **the verbatim sentence is not — do not put it in quotation marks.**

**What it means for a family, and it is not what they will assume:** a mollusc
allergy is real and can be severe, but **a US label need not warn about it**.
"Contains: shellfish" means *crustacean*. Molluscs may be declared voluntarily or
not at all, so the label shortcut that works for peanut and milk fails here — the
ingredient list must be read. Conversely, a child who reacted to shrimp has not
necessarily reacted to oyster. ⚠️ No verified source quantifies mollusc allergy
prevalence or crustacean↔mollusc cross-reactivity in infants — publish no
percentage.

## Raw shellfish — a hard prohibition at every age

FDA names **children** among at-risk groups who "should avoid… raw or undercooked
fish or shellfish… **raw oysters, even if they are treated after they have been
harvested.**"

CDC: "**You cannot tell that an oyster is bad by looking at it**… You can get sick
from eating oysters during **any month of the year**… **Hot sauce and lemon juice
don't kill *Vibrio*.**" On *V. vulnificus*: "**About 1 in 5 people with this
infection die**, sometimes within a day or two."

⚠️ **FDA does not define "children" numerically here** — don't attach the under-5
framing used elsewhere for *Salmonella*.

## Cooking — two rule sets, CDC is stricter

FDA: seafood to **145 °F**; molluscs — "the shells open during cooking — throw
out ones that don't open."

CDC, more conservative and the one to publish: discard any shellfish with open
shells **before** cooking; "boil until the shells open and **continue boiling for
another 3–5 minutes**", or steam 4–9 minutes; discard any that don't open fully.
Shucked oysters: boil ≥3 min, or fry ≥3 min at 375 °F, or broil 3 min, or bake at
450 °F for 10 min.

## Sodium — the number, and an honest caveat

NASEM 2019 DRI: sodium AI **110 mg/d (0–6m)**, **370 mg/d (7–12m)**, 800 mg/d
(1–3y). Same table: iron 0.27\*/11 mg, zinc 2\*/3 mg, iodine 110\*/130\* mcg.

⚠️ **There is no EAR and no UL for sodium at any age** — so 370 mg/day is *not* a
guideline-issued maximum, it is an adequacy target. It is nonetheless small enough
that one seasoned adult portion overwhelms it. **"Pull the baby's portion before
seasoning" is `common_practice`** — sound, widely advised, traceable to no
numbered rule.

## Sprouts — FDA names mung bean twice

> "sprouts (such as alfalfa or **mung bean**) that are served raw or lightly
> cooked may contain bacteria"… "**People who are more vulnerable to foodborne
> illness… should avoid eating raw or lightly cooked sprouts of any kind**
> (including onion, alfalfa, clover, radish, and mung bean sprouts)."

The vulnerable list opens with "**Children**". Pathogens named: *Salmonella*,
*Listeria*, *E. coli*. **"Lightly cooked" is prohibited too — a quick blanch is
not the mitigation.** "Of any kind" covers 콩나물, which FDA does not name.

## Iodine — the actionable fact is the variability

ODS: "commercially available seaweeds in whole or sheet form have iodine
concentrations ranging from **16 mcg/g to 2,984 mcg/g**." Excess iodine "can cause
some of the same symptoms as iodine deficiency — including goiter, elevated TSH…
and hypothyroidism", plus thyroiditis and thyroid papillary cancer.

**A 186-fold range in a single sentence is the publishable fact.** A parent cannot
infer the dose from a package. That, not a number, is what to say.

## Per food — no `fdcId` anywhere; composition figures only from ODS and the USDA iodine database

### Mussels 홍합
Age ~6m general (roster's 9m is editorial). **Mollusc — NOT a US-9 allergen.**
Choking **modify**: shuck completely, **pull the beard**, finger through for shell
chips, ≤½ in. Cook: discard non-closers, boil until open **+3–5 min**, discard
unopened. 🚩 **Mussel appears on NO FDA/EPA mercury list — not Best, not Good, not
Avoid. That is absence of assessment, not clearance.** Iron/zinc **unverified** —
conventionally cited as iron-rich, but ODS's iron table lists only oysters.

### Cooked oyster 굴 — the nutrient star and the biggest hazard
🏆 **"Oysters contain more zinc per serving than any other food"** — ODS says it
outright. Eastern raw 3 oz = **32 mg (291% DV)**; Pacific cooked = 28.2 mg.
Against a **3 mg/day** AI at 7–12 months, a very small portion does the work.
**Iron (heme): cooked 3 oz = 8 mg (44% DV)** against an 11 mg/day RDA — a genuinely
major source. **Iodine:** cooked 3 oz = 93 mcg; USDA iodine DB 109.1 mcg/100 g
(range 81.4–132). ⚠️ *Derived:* 100 g ≈ 84% of the 7–12m AI.
**Mercury: Best Choice, "even lower" sub-list.** Mollusc, not a US-9 allergen.
🚨 **Hard prohibition: raw oyster at any age, including post-harvest-treated.**
Deserves its own callout — raw oysters are a normal adult food and will be in the
house.

### Manila clam 바지락
**Grit is the defining prep problem** — purge in salted water (`common_practice`,
no guideline addresses it). Shell chips; ≤½ in. Same CDC cooking rule. Mollusc,
not US-9. **Mercury: Best Choice, "even lower" sub-list.** **Iodine:** canned
mixed-species proxy 66.5 mcg/100 g — *canned, not Manila, not fresh; label it.*
⚠️ **Iron unverified.** Clams are conventionally cited as among the densest iron
foods and **ODS's iron table does not list them** — this is exactly the case where
the well-known figure is the kind we're barred from supplying from memory.

### Scallop 가리비
**Choking — modify, and the mechanism is unusual: overcooking *creates* the
hazard.** Properly cooked is soft; overcooked turns dense and rubbery. FDA's
doneness test is "firm, pearly, and opaque" — stop there. ⚠️ A scallop is a squat
cylinder; applying CDC's "cut cylindrical foods into short thin strips, not
rounds" (written about hot dogs) is **defensible extrapolation, not issued** —
badge as inference, exactly as batch 1 did for carrot coins. Remove the side
muscle (`common_practice`). Mollusc, not US-9. Mercury: Best Choice, sub-list.
**Iodine 7.4 mcg/100 g — an order of magnitude below oyster and clam; scallop is
not an iodine concern.**

### Dried anchovy 멸치
**Allergen: YES — fish, a US-9.** FDA requires the *species* be declared.
🚩 **Direct conflict with a CDC rule, to disclose.** CDC: "remove all fat, skin
and **bones** from poultry, meat and fish before cooking." A dried anchovy *is*
the bones. **The only mitigation satisfying the rule is heads and viscera off and
ground to a powder, used as seasoning rather than eaten as a piece. Whole 멸치 as a
finger food is not defensible under CDC's own text.**
**Mercury: Best Choice, and named in the "even lower" sub-list** — one of the
safest fish on the chart, worth saying because small oily fish make parents
nervous. 🚩 Sodium: commercial dried anchovies are frequently salted; **no figure
obtainable — publish the AI and "buy unsalted, check the label", not a number.**

### Braised short rib 갈비찜
**Beef itself is ~6m and guideline-supported. 갈비찜 is a 12m item because of
간장, not the beef** — a family can serve the meat at 6m if they pull it before
seasoning. Make that distinction explicit.
🚩 **Two US-9 allergens via the sauce: soy, and — less obviously — wheat**, since
most brewed Korean and Japanese soy sauces contain it. ⚠️ No source quantifying
wheat or residual gluten in 간장 — instruct label-reading, publish no threshold.
🚩 **Two hazard-list entries: "tough or large chunks of meat" and "bones in meat
or fish."** CDC says remove bones *before* cooking, which 갈비찜 by definition does
not. **Take the meat off the bone after braising, shred along the grain, remove fat
and cartilage, ≤½ in. Never hand a baby the rib bone.**
Sodium is the governing constraint and **is not adjustable by dilution** — braise
the baby's portion separately or in unsalted stock. Iron is heme; **short-rib
specific figures unverified — do not borrow batch 1's ground-beef values.**

### Laver / gim 김
**Unseasoned, unsalted, un-oiled only.** Not a US-9 allergen — 🚩 **but seasoned
commercial 김 is very often finished with sesame seeds or oil, and sesame IS a
US-9.** A label-reading item.
**Iodine, verified:** USDA iodine DB Release 4 (Oct 2024), *nori, dried* =
**2,316.7 mcg/100 g** (range **1,640–2,850**). ODS: "2 tablespoons, flaked (5 g):
**116 mcg, 77% DV**." ⚠️ *Derived:* 5 g ≈ **89% of the entire 130 mcg daily AI for
a 7–12 month old**, from two tablespoons of flakes.
**Because there is no infant UL, "how much is too much" has no guideline answer.**
Honest framing: not a food to give freely or daily; a small amount is a large
fraction of the reference intake; the ±25% spread means even the same product
varies. Choking low, with an unverified caveat: a dry sheet can adhere to the
palate — **no body names this**, `common_practice`.

### Miyeok 미역
🚨 **The primary literature openly disagrees by up to two orders of magnitude.**
- **Dawczynski 2007** (*J Agric Food Chem*, ICP-MS, 34 dried products): "Brown
  algae contained as much as **1,316 ± 1,669 mg of I/kg FM**. More than **4,000
  mg/kg** were found in several *Laminaria* sp."
- **González 2020** (*Biol Trace Elem Res*, redox titration, 30 samples):
  "European kombu… **27.7 ± 5.4 mg/kg dry weight**"; 4 g/day ≈ 111 µg iodine.

**Roughly 50–150× apart**, different methods, different origins. **Report both;
do not average them and do not pick one.** ⚠️ **No 미역-specific figure was
verifiable** — Dawczynski's number is a three-genus brown-algae group mean, and
attributing it to 미역 alone would be invention.

**On frequency — the question the brief asked, answered honestly: no guideline
body (AAP, CDC, NHS, Health Canada, ESPGHAN, EFSA) addresses 미역국 for infants at
all.** A genuine gap, not something to paper over. Verified facts: no infant
iodine UL; 1–3y UL is 200 mcg; content unpredictable across a ~186-fold range;
excess causes goiter, elevated TSH, hypothyroidism. The inference — that occasional
미역국 with the seaweed strained is a different exposure from daily, and daily is
worth raising with a pediatrician — is **ours, and must be labelled so.**

### 다시마 dashima / kombu — not a roster food; name it as the outlier
Families use it for stock and will assume interchangeability.
**Miyai 2008** (*Endocr J*, normal Japanese adults): "Ingesting **15 and 30 g of
Kombu (iodine contents: 35 and 70 mg) daily**… significantly increased serum
**TSH**, exceeding normal limits in some subjects"; 15 g/day for 55–87 days kept
TSH elevated; baseline returned 7–40 days after stopping.
⚠️ *Derived:* 35 mg per 15 g ≈ **2,333 mcg/g**, versus **23.2 mcg/g** for nori —
**~100×.** Arithmetic across two sources with different methods; publish as an
order-of-magnitude statement, not a precise ratio.
**The brief's premise that 다시마 is the real outlier is correct and if anything
understated** — closer to two orders of magnitude, and the only seaweed here with
a human trial showing thyroid suppression at ordinary culinary amounts in adults.
**Inference (ours):** don't let a baby drink 다시마 stock routinely; strain the
kelp; don't reduce the stock, which concentrates it.

### Soybean sprouts 콩나물
🚩 **Allergen: YES — soybean is a US-9.** Easy to miss because it presents as a
vegetable, and it is why this differs from 숙주.
🚨 **Cooked thoroughly only** — FDA's "of any kind" governs even though soybean
isn't named. Choking **modify**: long fibrous strings, tough root tail — trim
tails, chop ≤½ in. Iron/zinc unverified.

### Mung bean sprouts 숙주
**Not a US-9 allergen** — mung bean is not soy, worth stating beside 콩나물 since
they look alike in a bowl. 🚨 **The one sprout FDA names twice** — the strongest
food-specific citation in this pack. Cooked thoroughly only; same string hazard.

## Where no Western guideline coverage exists at all

Stated plainly: **no body verified this session issues any guidance on 미역국,
갈비찜, 콩나물, 숙주, 멸치 or 김 as infant foods.** What exists is category-level —
mollusc → shellfish safety and non-listed allergen; 멸치 → fish allergen plus
mercury; sprouts → FDA's raw-sprout prohibition; 갈비찜 → beef, soy, bones, sodium;
김/미역 → iodine with no infant ceiling. **Every food-specific instruction here is
inherited category rule plus inference and must carry that label. The single
exception is 숙주, which FDA names by name.**

## Disagreements to surface, not resolve

- **Seaweed iodine:** Dawczynski vs González, ~50–150× apart. The cautious default is to treat seaweed iodine as **unknowable per package**.
- **Mollusc cooking stringency:** FDA stops at "shells open"; CDC requires 3–5 minutes past. **CDC is the cautious default.**
- **Anchovy vs CDC's bone rule:** grinding is the only reconciliation, and it is ours, not CDC's.
