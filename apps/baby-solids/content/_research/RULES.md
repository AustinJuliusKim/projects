# Research pack rules

Every research agent working on this canon follows these. They exist because
this content tells a parent what to put in a baby's mouth.

## Sources

**Primary only:** AAP / HealthyChildren, CDC, WHO, NHS, Health Canada, CPS,
NIAID, AAAAI, CSACI, FDA, EPA, EFSA, BfR, ESPGHAN, NIH ODS, USDA FoodData
Central, and peer-reviewed literature.

**Never fetch, quote, paraphrase or consult solidstarts.com or any competing
baby-feeding app or blog** — not for facts, not for phrasing, not for the shape
of a description. Their terms bar derivative works and the selection and
arrangement of their food set is protected. Skip such search results.

Every fact carries a URL and a retrieval date.

## Check that guidance is still live

**AAP retired its 2005 infant-methemoglobinemia clinical report in June 2025**,
and an earlier pack cited it as current. Before citing any policy or clinical
statement more than a few years old, check the live page for a retirement,
reaffirmation or replacement notice, and say which you found. A retired
statement is an absence of guidance, not a reversal of it — report it that way.

## Numbers that sound sourced but are not

**There is no issued "1:3" dilution ratio for nut or seed butters.** AAP issues
a qualitative rule only — "spread thinly." CDC names the hazard and issues no
fix. The one numeric preparation in US guidance is NIAID's, and it is
peanut-specific: 2 tsp peanut butter plus 2–3 tsp hot water, roughly 1:1 to
1:1.5. A "1:3" applied to almond, cashew or tahini is doubly extrapolated —
wrong ratio, wrong food. Publish the method ("thin until it pours off a spoon"),
not a borrowed number.

This one was introduced by *me* in an earlier brief and repeated downstream
before anyone checked it. Treat confident-sounding ratios in your own
instructions with the same suspicion as anything else.

**NIAID's age floors are stricter than AAP's** and are the cautious default:
whole nuts <5 years, peanut butter from a spoon or in lumps <4 years.

## Never invent a number

If a size, dose, gram amount, age, temperature or `fdcId` cannot be verified
from a primary source, write **unverified**. Do not approximate, interpolate,
or fill from your own knowledge. Every pack ends with an explicit register of
what it refused to verify. **This instruction outranks completeness** — a pack
covering eight foods honestly beats one covering eleven with two invented
figures.

Verify `fdcId`s live against fdc.nal.usda.gov. Quote no id you did not see
returned.

## Know which rules are actually issued

**AAP's "no piece larger than one-half inch" is the only dimensional rule any
US guideline body issues.** Every "finger-length", "two fingers wide" or
"thumbnail-sized" instruction is common practice. CDC deliberately refuses to
bind textures to month bands at all, so the 6-8 / 9-11 / 12-17 structure is the
app's editorial construct. Label accordingly.

Guideline-issued shape rules that *do* exist: cut cylindrical foods into short
thin strips not rounds; cut small spherical foods into small pieces; cook hard
vegetables and fruits until they mash easily; remove fat, skin and bones before
cooking; cook and grind whole grain kernels (all CDC). NHS adds: peel firm
produce, cut into narrow batons, or grate.

## Evidence tiers

`guideline` · `retired_guideline` · `trial` · `reference` (composition data
such as USDA — a measurement, not advice) · `expert_opinion` ·
`common_practice`.

## Report disagreements, don't resolve them

Where bodies differ — NHS permits lightly cooked Lion-stamped egg where FDA/CDC
prohibit undercooked egg; Health Canada permits cow's milk as a drink from 9
months where AAP/CDC say 12; the US and EU differ on nitrate — surface both
positions and say which is the more cautious default.

## Claims that are wrong and must not be repeated

- "Wait 3–5 days between new foods" — not evidence-based; IgE reactions occur
  within minutes to two hours.
- "Food before one is just for fun" — iron and zinc needs say otherwise.
- "Gagging means they aren't ready" — it's a working protective reflex.
- Any suggestion of broad allergy-panel screening — very low positive
  predictive value in infants.
- Unmodified BLW framing — the safety evidence is for the BLISS variant.
- "Oxalate blocks spinach iron" — a randomized crossover (Bonsmann 2008) found
  added potassium oxalate made no difference (P=0.86). Calcium and polyphenols
  are the real inhibitors.
