## Background

Salmon does two jobs at once: it is heme iron at 1.03 mg per 100 g — the readily
absorbed kind, like [[beef]] and [[chicken-thigh]] — and it is the food most
families use to introduce fish, one of the nine major US allergens.

On the allergen side, AAP's position is that there is no evidence delaying fish
past four to six months prevents allergy. The old advice to hold allergens back
was reversed for peanut on trial evidence, and the delay advice for the other
allergens went with it. Fish is introduced like any other food, with the medical
gate above for the babies who need it.

On mercury: FDA and EPA place salmon in the **Best Choices** category, and
within that in the sub-list of fish that are *even lower* in mercury. Among
common fish it is close to the safest choice you can make.

## Prep 6-8

Buy it boneless if you can, and go over it with your fingers anyway. Remove the
skin and any bones **before** cooking, which is CDC's instruction and NHS's
("no bones") — pin bones are far easier to find in a raw fillet than in a hot
cooked one.

Bake, poach, or pan-cook until the flesh is opaque all the way through and
flakes apart under a fork. Flake it fine, run the flakes through your fingers
one more time, and moisten with a spoonful of the cooking liquid or a puree so
it holds together instead of powdering.

Deboned salmon flesh is soft and low-risk. The bones are the entire hazard.

## Prep 9-11

Larger flakes, self-fed. Salmon stirred through [[oatmeal]] or mashed vegetables
is easier to gather than dry flakes on a tray.

## Prep 12-17

However the family eats it, still deboned and still cooked through. No raw fish
at this age.

## Safety

**Bones.** "Bones in meat or fish" is a named entry on CDC's choking-hazard
list. Debone before cooking, then check the cooked flakes by hand. Whole small
fish and bone-in steaks are not a form to serve here.

**Cooking.** Cook salmon through — opaque to the centre, flaking cleanly. No
raw, no smoked, no cured: none of those are cooked, and cured fish is
salt-preserved besides, where high-sodium foods are advised against under two
years. This record does not print a target internal temperature for fish: the
figure in general circulation could not be confirmed against USDA's own chart
while this was written, and we would rather give you the doneness test than a
number we cannot stand behind. Opaque through the middle, flaking cleanly under
a fork — that is what the temperature describes anyway.

**How much per week — there is no infant answer.** FDA and EPA's fish advice —
two servings a week from Best Choices, at a serving size of about one ounce for
a one-to-three year old — is written for **children ages 1 to 11**. It starts at
the first birthday. **No US body publishes a per-week amount for a six-to-eleven
month old**, so this record does not give one: any specific infant ounce figure
in circulation has been extrapolated by somebody, and we are not going to add
another. Serve salmon as one of several protein foods across the week, and if
you want an actual number for your own baby, ask your pediatrician.

For wild-caught fish from local waters, check your state or provincial
advisories — those are issued per water body and FDA's national list does not
replace them.
