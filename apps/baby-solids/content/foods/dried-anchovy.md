## Background

멸치 is the backbone of Korean home cooking — the stock under most soups, the
side dish stir-fried with soy and syrup, the handful eaten straight from the
bag. It is also the food in this batch that sits in the most direct conflict
with a rule an American guideline body actually issues, and we would rather show
you the conflict than quietly resolve it.

🚩 **CDC's instruction is to "remove all fat, skin and bones from poultry, meat
and fish before cooking." A dried anchovy *is* the bones.** There is no version
of a whole 멸치 that satisfies that sentence. The whole skeleton, the head, the
spine — that is the food.

**The only preparation that reconciles the two is this: heads and viscera off,
the fish ground to a fine powder, and the powder used as a seasoning stirred
into food rather than eaten as a piece.** Ground finely enough, there is no bone
left to remove because there is no intact bone left at all. **Whole 멸치 as a
finger food is not defensible under CDC's own text**, and this record does not
recommend it at any age in its range — which is why all three age bands below
say the same thing.

That reconciliation is **ours, not CDC's.** CDC names the rule; it does not
offer this fix, or any fix, for small whole fish. You are entitled to know which
part came from the guideline and which part came from us.

**Mercury is the good news, and it is worth saying out loud because small oily
fish make parents nervous.** FDA and EPA place anchovy in **Best Choices**, and
within that on the sub-list of fish that are *even lower* in mercury. It is one
of the safest fish on the entire chart — the opposite of the tuna-and-swordfish
worry that the word "fish" tends to trigger. Small, short-lived, low on the food
chain.

**Fish is one of the nine major US allergens**, and unlike molluscs it is fully
covered by labelling law — FDA requires the **species** to be declared, so a
package will tell you it is anchovy. See the medical gate above before a first
exposure.

**Composition is pending verification.** The USDA database was unavailable when
this record was researched, so there is no iron, calcium or sodium figure here.
Dried anchovy is conventionally described as a calcium food precisely because
you eat the skeleton, and that is plausible, but we could not check it and are
not going to print an unverified number.

## Prep 6-8

**Buy unsalted, and read the label to confirm it.** This is the single most
important purchasing decision for this food, and it is easy to get wrong:
commercial dried anchovies are very often salted, and there is no way to tell by
looking. Products vary enormously and **we could not obtain a sodium figure for
any of them**, so this record gives none.

What it can give you is the scale of a baby's whole day. Sodium adequate intake
is **110 mg a day from birth to six months** and **370 mg a day from seven to
twelve months** (NASEM 2019). Note carefully what that is and is not: there is
**no upper limit and no EAR for sodium at any age**, so 370 mg is an adequacy
target, not a ceiling anyone issued. It is nonetheless small enough that a
salted adult ingredient can overwhelm it in one go.

Then:

1. **Pull the heads off** and pinch out the dark viscera along the belly. On the
   small stock anchovies this takes seconds each.
2. **Toast them briefly** in a dry pan to drive off moisture — this makes them
   grind properly instead of turning gummy.
3. **Grind to a fine powder** in a spice grinder or mortar. Fine means fine: no
   fragments you can feel between your fingers.
4. **Stir a small amount into food the baby already eats** — [[oatmeal]], soft
   rice, mashed [[sweet-potato]], a vegetable puree.

Store the powder airtight in the fridge and make small batches; it goes stale
quickly.

## Prep 9-11

No change. The powder is still the form, for the same reason: a pincer grasp
makes it *easier* for a baby to pick up a whole dried anchovy, which is exactly
what we do not want. Better hand skills do not make a skeleton chewable.

You can be more generous with where the powder goes — through vegetables,
stirred into rice porridge, mixed into a patty — but it stays a seasoning.

## Prep 12-17

Still ground. A toddler can chew far more than an infant, but "bones in meat or
fish" remains a named entry on CDC's choking-hazard list past the first
birthday, and a whole dried anchovy is nothing but bones.

The other thing that arrives at this age is the family version of the dish —
멸치볶음, stir-fried with 간장 and syrup. That is a sodium-and-sugar dish rather
than a fish dish; if you want the child to eat 멸치, the powder is still the way,
and the stir-fry can wait.

**Stock is a partial exception worth understanding.** Anchovies simmered and
then *strained out* leave a broth rather than a food — the fish never reaches
the baby. That is fine as a cooking base as long as the broth is not salted. It
is the anchovies themselves, left in and eaten, that this record is about.

## Safety

**The bone conflict is the headline.** CDC says remove all bones from fish
before cooking. A dried anchovy is the bones. Grinding to a powder is the only
preparation that satisfies the rule, and it is our reconciliation, not CDC's.
Whole 멸치 is not a finger food here at any age.

**Sodium is the second hazard and it is a shopping problem, not a cooking
problem.** Buy unsalted and verify on the label. You cannot rinse or dilute salt
back out of a dried fish.

**Mercury is not a hazard here** — Best Choices, and on the *even lower*
sub-list.

**Fish allergy** is managed by the medical gate above, then by ordinary early
introduction. Watch for about two hours after a first exposure; IgE reactions
happen within minutes to two hours. The familiar "wait three to five days
between new foods" rule adds nothing diagnostically.

**No guideline body addresses 멸치 as an infant food.** What exists is
category-level: fish as a major allergen, anchovy's mercury rating, and CDC's
bone rule. The grinding, the head-and-viscera removal and the seasoning-not-a-
piece framing are inherited category rules plus our inference, and are labelled
that way above.
