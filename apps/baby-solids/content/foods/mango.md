## Background

A ripe mango is about as easy as first foods get: soft, sweet, and mashable
without cooking. Neither AAP nor CDC names mango individually — NHS covers it at
the category level as a soft ripe fruit — so its place here is category-level
coverage rather than a food-specific ruling.

**Vitamin C varies by cultivar by roughly fivefold**, so this record gives a
range rather than a number: USDA's verified entries run from **25.5 mg per 100 g
for Tommy Atkins to 168 mg for Ataulfo**, with the generic entry at 36.4 mg. Any
of those makes mango a genuinely useful vitamin-C partner for a plant-iron meal
— [[tofu]], [[green-peas]], [[barley]] cereal — since vitamin C improves
absorption of non-heme iron eaten alongside it. Mango's own iron is a trace
0.16 mg per 100 g, and the two Foundation cultivar samples measured zero.

Peel it and take the stone out. NHS's instruction is to always remove hard pips
or stones, and a mango stone is large, fibrous, and exactly the wrong shape.

## Prep 6-8

Pick a mango that gives under gentle pressure and smells sweet at the stem end.
Peel it, cut the two cheeks off either side of the flat stone, then cut
finger-length strips.

Ripe mango is genuinely slippery, which is the main practical problem at this
age. Two things that help: leave a band of peel at one end as a handle while
keeping the eating end bare, or roll the strip in a little infant cereal or
ground oats for grip. Neither is a safety measure — they are grip aids.

An underripe mango is firm. Do not serve that raw in strips: cook it soft or
grate it, exactly as with raw [[apple]].

## Prep 9-11

With the pincer grasp — most often from 9 to 10 months per AAP — soft mango
cubes about the size of your thumbnail work well, with a couple of larger strips
alongside so biting stays in practice.

Mashed mango is also a good carrier for something less popular: stirred into
plain [[yogurt]], or over [[barley]] cereal.

## Prep 12-17

However the rest of the table is eating it, peeled and stoned. Cubes, strips,
blended into a lassi with plain yogurt and no added sugar.

## Safety

The stone is the hazard, and it is the only one that comes with an actual
guideline instruction: remove hard pips and stones. Do not hand over a gnawed
stone as a teether — it is the wrong size and shape, and the fibres come away.

**Skin contact, not food allergy.** Mango peel, leaves and stem contain
urushiol-family compounds — the same chemical family as poison ivy — and
peer-reviewed dermatology literature documents type-IV *contact* dermatitis on
the lips and face in people already sensitised to poison ivy or oak. Two things
about that. First, it is not a food allergy and not an IgE reaction; it is a
delayed rash from touching the peel. Second, prior sensitisation is very
unlikely in an infant who has never met poison ivy. This is peer-reviewed
literature, **not** a pediatric guideline, and no body issues advice about it.

The practical consequence is small and you were going to do it anyway: serve
peeled flesh, and keep the peel off the tray.
