## Background

**The frustrating thing about lotus root is that the nutrition is good and the
texture disqualifies it.** Cooked, it carries **27.4 mg of vitamin C per 100 g —
the highest of any food in this part of the canon** — plus 0.90 mg of iron and
useful fibre. On paper it looks like an excellent vitamin-C partner for an iron
meal.

It does not pass the one texture rule that matters. CDC's instruction is to
"cook food until it is soft enough to easily mash with a fork." **Lotus root
cannot be cooked to that point in a home kitchen.** Its crispness is structural
rather than a matter of doneness: the rhizome is full of air channels
(aerenchyma), and its cell walls are lignified and cross-linked with calcium, so
they hold together through boiling, braising and long stewing. That is not a
defect — it is exactly why the root is prized in Korean, Chinese and Japanese
cooking. 연근 is served *because* it stays crunchy.

Compare it with the other roots in this batch. **[[potato]], [[taro]] and
[[cassava]] all pass the fork-mash test after ordinary cooking. Lotus root does
not, at any household cooking time.** Two of those three carry real toxicology
warnings and are still easier to serve safely than this one, because their
hazard can be cooked or peeled away and lotus root's cannot.

Its culinary form makes it worse rather than better: the standard cut is a hard
round slice, and a firm round is the shape AAP singles out. And 연근조림, the
most common household preparation, braises it in soy sauce and sugar — added
sugars and sodium, both of which CDC puts off the menu under 24 months.

**So the ruling here is: lotus root is not appropriate as a finger food before
12 months, in any size or shape, and this record's `firstOkMonths` of 12 is for
lotus root as its own food.** Before then, the only defensible use is finely
grated or puréed into something else, where it contributes very little.

**This is an editorial judgement, not a guideline.** No guideline body names
lotus root at all. What is issued is CDC's fork-mash rule; applying it to this
root is a derivation, and it is labelled as one throughout. If you disagree, you
are disagreeing with our reading rather than with CDC. A human reviewer should
ratify this before it is treated as settled.

## Prep 12-17

**Finely grated or puréed into another food.** Peel it, grate it on the fine side
of a box grater, and cook it into a porridge, a soup, or a pancake batter, where
it disappears into the texture of whatever it is in. That is the form that works,
and it is the same form as under 12 months — the age band does not change the
root.

**What not to do:** slices, half-moons, batons or chunks, no matter how long they
have been braised. A braised slice of 연근 that an adult reads as "soft" is still
crisp against a toddler's gums and molars, and it is round and firm, which is the
shape both AAP and CDC warn about.

Skip 연근조림 for this age even in small pieces. The soy sauce and sugar are
separate problems from the texture, and both are on CDC's under-24-month list.

If what you want is the vitamin C, [[broccoli]], [[potato]], [[mango]] or
[[plantain]] deliver it in a form that mashes.

## Safety

**Lotus root is not a finger food.** That is the whole safety note, and it is the
one thing worth remembering from this record.

It fails CDC's fork-mash rule structurally, not because of undercooking, so
"cook it longer" is not the fix — there is no cooking time at which a crisp
rhizome becomes mashable. Its standard cut is a firm round, which is the shape
AAP explicitly warns about for children under four, and its air channels mean a
slice holds together as a ring rather than collapsing.

**This assessment is derived from a general CDC rule, not issued by any body
about this food, and is badged accordingly.** It is the most restrictive call in
this batch and the least externally supported one, which is precisely why it is
labelled as an opinion rather than dressed up as guidance. A human should
review it.
