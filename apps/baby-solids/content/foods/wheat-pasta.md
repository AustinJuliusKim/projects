## Background

Pasta is the **low-sodium** way to cover wheat. Cooked whole-grain pasta runs
about **6 mg of sodium per 100 g**; wheat toast runs 473 mg untoasted and 601
toasted. If you are covering the wheat allergen and the sodium in
[[wheat-toast]] bothers you — it should — this is the same allergen at a
hundredth of the salt. Iron is 1.65 mg per 100 g, non-heme, so pair it with a
vitamin-C food in the same meal: [[broccoli]], [[green-peas]], a tomato sauce.

**Check the label before the first exposure, because pasta frequently contains
egg.** Fresh pasta almost always does, "egg noodles" say so, and plenty of dried
pastas include it without making a point of it. **Egg is a second major
allergen**, and if it arrives unannounced inside a first wheat exposure you have
introduced two allergens simultaneously and learned nothing useful from either.
It is on the label — US law requires egg to be declared — so it takes ten
seconds to check. See [[egg]] for how to introduce it deliberately.

**On iron, a gap worth knowing about.** The figure on this record is for
whole-grain pasta. **Enriched** pasta — most standard white pasta in the US — is
meaningfully higher in iron because iron is added back, but no verified USDA
entry for it was available while this was written, so no enriched figure appears
here. Do not read 1.65 mg as the ceiling for all pasta; read it as the one number
that could be stood behind.

Wheat is one of the nine major allergens. Barley and rye contain gluten but are
not major allergens, and "gluten-free" is not the same claim as "wheat-free" —
[[barley]] covers that distinction, and the separate question of celiac disease,
in full.

## Prep 6-8

**Cook it well past al dente.** Whatever the box says, add several minutes, and
test by squashing a piece between two fingers — it should give completely. CDC's
standard for infant food is that it mashes easily, and pasta cooked to an
adult's bite does not: it stays springy and slick, which is the worst pair of
properties to hand a baby with no molars.

**Short shapes beat long strands.** A single elbow, a wide flat piece, a large
shell — something the whole hand can hold and the gums can crush. If you only
have spaghetti, cut it short after cooking. CDC's shape rule is to cut
cylindrical foods into short thin strips rather than rounds, and a long strand
of pasta is the one form that invites a baby to take in more than they can
manage.

**No salt in the cooking water.** This is the step people skip because it feels
like it belongs to the pot rather than the baby. Salted water is how a
6 mg-per-100 g food stops being one.

Toss the cooked pasta with a little of its water, or a plain sauce, so it does
not stick to itself in a clump.

## Prep 9-11

Smaller shapes for the pincer grasp, still cooked soft, still no piece larger
than half an inch. Pasta with a mashed vegetable or a soft protein stirred
through is easier to gather than bare pieces that slide around the tray.

Still no salt in the water, and be careful with jarred sauces at this age — they
are usually where the sodium arrives once the pasta itself has stopped
contributing any.

## Prep 12-17

However the household eats it, cooked softer than the adults would choose, and
cut down. Long strands can come in around now with supervision, cut into short
lengths.

## Safety

**Pasta is not a named entry on any official choking list.** The instructions
here are applications of CDC's general rules — cook until it mashes easily, cut
cylindrical foods into short thin strips — plus AAP's half-inch maximum. That is
category-level coverage and this record says so rather than implying a
pasta-specific ruling exists.

**Undercooked pasta is the actual hazard.** Al dente is a texture designed for
teeth. A firm, curved, slippery piece is exactly the profile that lodges.

**Egg is the allergen that arrives by surprise.** Read the ingredients, not the
shape of the noodle.

**Salt is the thing you control.** No salt in the water, and check the sauce.
