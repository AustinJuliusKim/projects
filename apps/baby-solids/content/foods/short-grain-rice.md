## Background

Rice is the staple grain in a lot of households, this one included, and there is
no reason a baby cannot eat it. There are two things worth understanding before
you make it the default, and neither is a reason to panic.

**Arsenic, stated plainly.** Rice takes up more inorganic arsenic from soil and
water than other grains. FDA set an action level of **100 ppb** of inorganic
arsenic for infant rice cereal — draft in 2016, **finalized in August 2020, and
still live** with no retirement or replacement notice as of today. FDA's
reasoning is about dose relative to body size: "Relative to body weight, rice
intake for infants… is about **three times greater than for adults**," and
"people consume the most rice (relative to their weight) at approximately
**8 months of age**."

Eight months sits inside the 9–11 band on this app and just past the 6–8 one.
**The peak exposure is not somewhere off in toddlerhood — it is right in the
middle of the window this record covers**, which is why the advice below is
about variety rather than avoidance.

CDC's wording is the practical version: "**Only feeding your child infant rice
cereal can increase their risk of arsenic exposure**," and it recommends
offering oat, barley and multigrain cereals as well. Note what that says and
what it does not: the concern is a rice-only diet, not rice.

**Enriched or not, and it is a sevenfold difference.** Cooked short-grain white
rice that has been enriched carries **1.46 mg of iron per 100 g**. The same rice
unenriched carries **0.20 mg**. Enrichment is a US labelling practice; **most
rice sold in Korean markets is unenriched**, and the bag will not necessarily
make that obvious. If rice is a daily food in your house, this is the single
biggest nutritional variable in it, and neither number comes close to
[[fortified-infant-cereal]] — check the panel rather than assuming.

## Prep 6-8

Cook it much wetter and longer than you would for the table — the porridge end
of the range, where the grains have broken down and it spoons rather than
scatters. Then mash it. Juk made with extra water, or rice cooked and blended
with the cooking liquid, is the right texture here.

Stir in something with vitamin C, since rice iron is non-heme and absorbs
better alongside it: mashed [[broccoli]], [[potato]], or fruit.

**Do not make rice cereal the only cereal.** CDC names oat, barley and
multigrain as the alternatives, and the reason is arsenic dose over time, not
any single meal. Rotating cereals costs you nothing.

## Prep 9-11

Softly cooked rice, mashed, and mixed into other foods rather than served loose.
Short-grain rice sticks to itself, which is convenient for a baby learning to
pick things up and is also the thing to watch: break up any clump before it goes
on the tray. **No guideline body specifies a size for a rice clump**, so there
is no number to give you — squash it flat and it stops being a lump.

This is the age FDA's intake data peaks at. It is also the age iron matters
most, so if rice is displacing an iron food, that is the trade to look at.

## Prep 12-17

Rice as the family eats it, kept soft. Sticky rice shaped into balls is a common
finger food and a reasonable one at this age, as long as the ball squashes
easily and is small enough to bite rather than swallow whole.

## Safety

**Cooked whole grain kernels are a named CDC choking hazard**, and the issued
instruction is to cook and then finely grind or mash them. Rice grains are small,
but the rule is written about kernels rather than about size, and sticky rice
adds the clumping problem on top.

**The cooking trade-off — and both halves are real.** FDA says boiling rice
pasta-style in 6 to 10 parts water and draining it "can reduce **40 to 60
percent** of the inorganic arsenic." FDA also says in the same breath that doing
this "**also results in lowering the nutritional value… reduces the levels of
folate, iron, niacin and thiamine… by 50 to 70 percent**." Rinsing is not a
middle path: FDA says it "has a minimal effect on the arsenic" and "will wash
off iron, folate, thiamine and niacin."

So for a baby at risk of iron deficiency — which is most babies from about six
months — **these two pull in opposite directions, and FDA does not reconcile
them.** Neither will this record. What is left is the thing both agencies
actually recommend: rotate the grain. Serving oat or barley cereal on some days
lowers arsenic exposure without removing any iron at all.

**Rice drinks: the NHS prohibits them under five years old** — "Children under 5
years old should not have rice drinks as a substitute for breast milk, infant
formula or cows' milk as they may contain too much arsenic." That is the NHS's
position, and it is cited here as the NHS's. **No US body issues an equivalent
statement naming rice drinks**, so do not read this as a US rule; read it as the
more cautious of two positions, and a well-reasoned one.
