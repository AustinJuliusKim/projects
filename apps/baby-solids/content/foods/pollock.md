## Background

On mercury, pollock is about as safe as a fish gets in US guidance: FDA and EPA
put it in **Best Choices** *and* on the shorter sub-list of Best Choices that are
**even lower in mercury**. [[cod]] and canned light tuna are Best Choices without
being on that sub-list; pollock is on it, alongside [[rainbow-trout]] and
[[salmon]].

Its iron is the lowest of the five fish here — 0.29 mg per 100 g for Alaska
pollock, 0.59 mg for Atlantic — with 0.44 mg of zinc. So this is not an iron
food. What it is: an extremely mild white fish with a soft, forgiving flake, in
the safest mercury classification FDA issues, which makes it a sensible fish to
introduce early and a sensible one to repeat.

Fish is one of the nine major US allergens. AAP's position is that there is no
evidence delaying fish past four to six months prevents allergy, and FDA states
directly that fish can be introduced at about six months of age.

## Prep 6-8

Buy a plain frozen or fresh fillet and check the ingredient list — see the
sodium note in Safety, which is the thing most likely to go wrong with this
particular fish.

Skin and bones come off **before** cooking: CDC's order, NHS says to remove bones
from fish, and the Canadian Paediatric Society says plainly not to feed a baby
fish with bones. Go over the raw fillet with your fingers even if it was sold
boneless.

Bake, poach, or steam until the flesh is opaque through and flakes under light
pressure. USDA FSIS gives 145 °F for fish and shellfish. Pollock overcooks
quickly and goes rubbery, so check it early.

Flake fine, search the flakes by hand once more, and moisten with the cooking
liquid or a puree so they hold together rather than powdering.

## Prep 9-11

Larger flakes, self-fed, still hand-searched. Pollock's mildness makes it a good
carrier — stirred into [[oatmeal]], rice, or mashed vegetables it is easy to
gather and does not dominate the dish.

## Prep 12-17

However the family eats it, still deboned, still cooked through, and still from
an untreated fillet. Breaded fish sticks and crab sticks are different products
and the salt is in the coating and the binder rather than the fish.

## Safety

**The sodium trap is a purchasing decision, and it happens before any
seasoning.** Plain cooked Alaska pollock carries **166 mg of sodium per 100 g**.
Pollock treated with moisture-retention additives — sold as fillets that have
been soaked in a salt-and-phosphate solution to hold water through freezing —
carries **419 mg per 100 g**, and that is the fish as it comes, before you add a
single thing to the pan. Both figures are USDA-verified. CDC advises against
high-sodium foods for children under two.

Treated fillets look identical to untreated ones. The tell is the ingredient
list: a plain fillet's ingredients are "pollock". Anything naming sodium
tripolyphosphate, salt, or "solution" is the other kind. Read the back of the
bag.

**The traditional Korean pollock preparations are all excluded, and on salt
rather than on mercury.** 명태 is the same fish, but almost every way it is
preserved makes it a salt product:

- **북어 and 황태** — dried and freeze-dried pollock
- **코다리** — semi-dried
- **자반** — salted
- **명란** — salt-cured roe

These are the forms most likely to be in a Korean kitchen already, which is
exactly why they are worth naming. **We publish no sodium figure for any of them**
— no USDA record or primary-source figure was verified for these preparations
while this record was written, and a made-up number on a page about a baby's food
would be worse than none. The qualitative position is enough to act on: they are
salt-preserved, salt is advised against for babies, and none of them belongs in
this age range. Rehydrating or rinsing them is not a fix; the salt is throughout
the flesh, which is what preserved it.

**Surimi is a separate product.** Crab sticks, 맛살 and fish cake are made from
pollock but they are formed, salted, starch-bound foods rather than fish
fillets, and nothing on this page applies to them.

**Bones.** Deboned pollock flesh is soft and low-risk; the bones are the entire
hazard. Debone before cooking, then check the cooked flakes by hand. The tweezers
technique is common practice — the issued rule is that the bones come out, and
that they come out before cooking.

**No raw fish.** FDA is explicit that children should not eat raw fish,
including sushi and sashimi.

**How much per week — there is no infant answer.** FDA and EPA's advice is titled
for children **ages 1 to 11**, and its smallest figure is one ounce at age one.
**No US body publishes a per-week amount for a six-to-eleven-month-old**, so this
record does not invent one. Pollock's position on the "even lower in mercury"
sub-list means it is among the fish that classification was written to
encourage, not restrict — but "encouraged" is still not a number, and we are not
going to supply one.

**One wording point, because two rules look identical and are not.** FDA's fish
advice suggests watching your child for **several days before feeding fish a
second time**, while the same paragraph explicitly rejects delaying allergen
introduction. That sentence is about the gap between the **first and second
exposure to fish itself**. It is **not** the familiar "wait 3–5 days between new
foods" rule, which is about *unrelated* foods and is not evidence-based: IgE
reactions happen within minutes to two hours. Keep introducing other new foods on
your normal schedule. The tension between FDA's two sentences is real and we are
flagging it rather than resolving it.
