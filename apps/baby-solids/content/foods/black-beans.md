## Background

A cheap, storable, iron-carrying protein that both NHS and the Canadian
Paediatric Society name for this age — CPS's phrase is "mashed beans, peas or
lentils," and the mashing is not decoration.

The iron is non-heme, 2.10 mg per 100 g cooked, which means absorption depends
on what is on the plate beside it. Black beans contain **zero vitamin C**, so
they are the clearest case in this canon for deliberate pairing: CDC advises
serving non-heme iron with a vitamin-C food and names broccoli among its
vitamin-C foods. **[[broccoli]] with black beans is a pairing CDC's own advice
supports**, not a folk tip. [[lentils]] carry more iron per 100 g if you are
choosing between pulses.

## Prep 6-8

Cook from dry until the beans collapse, or use canned. Then flatten every single
bean — press each one between thumb and forefinger, or run a fork or potato
masher through the lot until nothing round is left. Loosen with cooking liquid,
unsalted broth, or a puree so the mash gathers on a spoon.

Bean skins sometimes separate and float free of the mash. No guideline body
instructs removing them; parents who do it are following common practice, not a
rule.

## Prep 9-11

Same mash, now self-fed, and it works well spread on a strip of toast or stirred
into [[oatmeal]]. A pincer grasp can pick up an individual bean, which is exactly
the reason to keep flattening them.

## Prep 12-17

Still flattened. This is the part parents are surprised by: "whole beans" is on
CDC's hazard list without an age qualifier, and a toddler who bolts food is not
obviously safer with a round bean than a nine-month-old. Refried, mashed into
rice, or squashed into a burrito filling all work.

## Safety

**Whole beans are a direct entry on the CDC/WIC choking-hazard list**, alongside
whole grapes and chunks of meat. They are small, round, smooth, and about the
size of an infant airway, and being soft does not change the geometry. Mashing
or flattening is the required modification, not a texture preference.

**Sodium, if you use canned.** Beans cooked from dry are about 1 mg of sodium per
100 g. Canned beans are substantially higher — this record does not publish a
figure because we could not verify one, so read the can. Rinsing canned beans
under running water is common practice for cutting the sodium; it is not
guideline-issued, and a no-salt-added can is the more reliable route.
