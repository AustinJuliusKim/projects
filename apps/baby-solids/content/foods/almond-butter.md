## Background

Almond butter is one of three tree-nut records in this canon, alongside
[[cashew-butter]] and [[ground-walnut]]. They are three records rather than one
because **tree nut is not a single allergen.** Almond and cashew are separate
sensitizations, and tolerating one says nothing at all about the other. Reported
clustering runs cashew–pistachio and hazelnut–walnut; almond does not cluster
with cashew.

Which leaves an honest gap that this record will not paper over: **no US body
issues a multi-tree-nut introduction protocol.** How many tree nuts you need to
"cover" the tree-nut allergen, in what order, at what interval — none of that is
issued by anyone. The specialist guidance that exists describes the evidence base
as sparse and waits on trial results. So this record tells you how to serve
almond butter safely, and stops short of telling you how many nuts make a
programme, because nobody knows.

**On the numbers: this record deliberately publishes none.** There is a verified
USDA entry for *whole almonds* — 3.71 mg of iron per 100 g — and it is listed in
the sources as a proxy so you can see what the raw nut looks like. It is **not**
printed as almond butter's iron, and no USDA id is attached to this record.
Grinding a nut into butter changes its moisture and therefore its per-100 g
density, so carrying a whole-nut figure on a butter record would be a
fabrication with a citation stapled to it. **Almond butter's own composition is
unverified here.**

## Prep 6-8

Use **smooth** almond butter, never crunchy, and check the label for a plain
one: nut and maybe salt, and preferably not even the salt.

Put about a quarter teaspoon in a bowl and stir in warm water, breast milk,
formula, or a puree the baby already eats, a little at a time, until **it no
longer holds its shape and pours off the spoon.** That is the test, and it is
deliberately a test rather than a ratio.

**There is no issued dilution ratio, and the specific one you may have seen
quoted for nut butters is issued by nobody.** AAP's rule is qualitative — spread
thinly. CDC names the hazard and offers no fix at all. The one numeric
preparation anywhere in US guidance belongs to NIAID's peanut-allergy-prevention
protocol: it is **written for peanut**, it is an allergy-dosing instruction
rather than a choking rule, and it does not transfer to almond. A borrowed
number here would be the wrong ratio applied to the wrong food. Thin it until it
pours, and you have done what the guidance actually supports.

Offer it in the morning, at home, on a day you are not rushing. That is a
logistics choice, not a medical one: it means you are watching, and your clinic
is open. Watch for about two hours — IgE reactions happen within minutes to two
hours.

## Prep 9-11

Same thinned form, or spread very thinly on something soft. **Thickness, not
quantity, is the hazard.** Nut butter stiff enough to hold its shape can
obstruct an airway, and a stiff quarter-teaspoon is more dangerous than a thin
tablespoon.

## Prep 12-17

Thinned into [[oatmeal]] or [[yogurt]], or spread very thinly on a strip of
[[wheat-toast]]. Still never off a spoon, and still never a thick layer.

## Safety

**This food is on the hazard list by name.** AAP's entry reads "thick chunks of
peanut butter or **other nut butters**" — the words "other nut butters" are what
make it bind almond butter directly, not by analogy. CDC's parallel entry is
"chunks or spoonful of nut and seed butters."

**Never crunchy.** A crunchy butter is a nut butter with pieces of nut in it,
and pieces of nut are separately on the hazard list.

**Never off a spoon.** NIAID's instruction not to give nut butter directly from
a spoon or in lumps to a child under four is written for peanut; applying it to
almond butter is this record's judgement, not NIAID's ruling, and it is tiered
as expert opinion for that reason. The physical mechanism is identical, which is
why it is worth applying.

**Whole almonds are a different food and are not appropriate here.** NIAID:
whole nuts should not be given under **5 years**. AAP's general framing is "until
4 years of age or older." **The two bodies disagree, both positions are live, and
NIAID's five-year floor is the cautious default.** This record does not pick one
silently — it tells you they differ. Whichever you follow, almond butter thinned
is fine from six months; the age floor is about the nut, not the butter. See
[[ground-walnut]] for why "chopped fine" does not get you out of that rule.
