## Background

Scallop is the one food on this list where **overcooking creates the hazard.**
That is backwards from almost everything else you have been told about feeding a
baby, where longer cooking means softer and safer — cook the carrot until it
mashes, cook the meat until it shreds. A scallop does the opposite. Properly
cooked it is soft and yields easily. Cooked past that point the proteins tighten
and it turns dense and rubbery, and a dense rubbery disc of protein is a much
harder thing for a baby to manage than a tender one.

So the instruction is not "cook it thoroughly", it is **cook it to the doneness
test and stop.** FDA gives the test in words for exactly this group of foods:
shrimp, scallops, crab and lobster are done when **"the flesh becomes firm,
pearly, and opaque."** That is the whole target. There is no benefit past it and
there is a real cost.

**Composition figures are not in this record.** The USDA database was
unavailable when it was researched, so there is no verified iron or protein
value to publish and none is printed. Shellfish flesh carries **heme** iron —
that is a classification, not a measurement.

**Iodine is genuinely not a concern here**, which is worth stating because it is
a concern for other foods in this batch. USDA's iodine database gives scallop
**7.4 µg per 100 g** — an order of magnitude below [[oyster]] and
[[manila-clam]], and several orders below [[gim]]. Scallop is not part of the
iodine conversation.

**On allergy: scallops are molluscs, and molluscs are not among the nine US
major allergens.** The nine name **crustacean** shellfish — crab, lobster,
shrimp — not scallop, oyster, clam or mussel. FDA's January 2025 final guidance
describes how it evaluates allergens outside the list. The consequence for a
family is that **a US label need not warn about molluscs**: "Contains: shellfish"
means crustacean, so the ingredient list has to be read. And a child who reacted
to shrimp has not necessarily reacted to scallop.

## Prep 6-8

**Remove the side muscle first** — the small tough tab on the side of the
adductor, sometimes called the foot. It peels off with your fingers. It is
chewier than the rest and does not soften. Removing it is ordinary practice, not
a rule anyone issues.

**Cook to the test, not to a timer.** Sear, poach or steam until the flesh is
**firm, pearly, and opaque** — FDA's words — and take it off the heat at that
moment. A scallop goes from tender to rubbery in well under a minute. If you are
cooking for adults at the same time and want a hard sear, cook the baby's one
separately and more gently; you cannot undo the toughening.

**Then cut it into short thin strips, across the round, rather than serving it
as a disc.** Keep every piece under half an inch.

⚠️ **A note on where that cutting rule comes from, because it is not issued for
this food.** CDC's instruction is to cut *cylindrical* foods into short thin
strips instead of rounds, and CDC wrote that rule about hot dogs. A scallop is a
squat cylinder of firm protein, so applying it here is a **defensible
extrapolation — an inference of ours, not a guideline instruction.** We think it
is the right call and we would rather you know it is an inference than assume
CDC issued it. The half-inch limit *is* issued: that one is AAP's.

## Prep 9-11

Same cooking, same side-muscle removal, same doneness test. With a pincer grasp
the strips can be a little shorter and thicker. The failure mode at this age is
not the size, it is a scallop that was cooked while you were doing something
else and came off the pan tough — check it by pressing one between your fingers
before it goes on the tray. It should give.

## Prep 12-17

However the household is eating them, still cooked no further than opaque, still
cut down rather than served whole. A whole seared scallop is a smooth firm round
about the size of an airway — it is not a form to hand over at this age, however
soft it is.

Take the child's portion out before butter, soy sauce or a pan sauce goes in.

## Safety

**The unusual one: overcooking is the hazard.** Stop at firm, pearly and opaque.
A tough scallop is harder for a baby to break down than a tender one, and no
amount of cutting fully compensates for the texture.

**Shape.** Not rounds. Short thin strips, nothing over half an inch. The strip
rule is our extrapolation from CDC's cylindrical-food instruction; the half-inch
limit is AAP's and is issued.

**The side muscle** is a small tough piece worth taking off.

**Raw is out**, as with every shellfish here — FDA names children among the
groups who should avoid raw or undercooked fish and shellfish. That includes
scallop crudo and 회.

**Mercury is not a concern.** FDA and EPA place scallops in **Best Choices**, on
the sub-list of choices that are *even lower* in mercury.

**Watch for "wet" scallops.** Scallops are often treated with a phosphate
solution that makes them hold water; they weigh more, they steam rather than
sear, and they are harder to cook to the right texture. Dry-packed scallops
behave more predictably. This is a cooking observation, not a safety finding,
and no guideline body addresses it.

**No guideline body addresses scallop as an infant food.** The doneness test,
the mercury rating and the half-inch limit are issued. The strip shape, the side
muscle and the stop-cooking-early emphasis are inference and ordinary practice,
labelled as such above.
