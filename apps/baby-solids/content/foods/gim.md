## Background

김 is one of the easiest foods in the Korean repertoire to hand a baby, and one
of the easiest to over-serve without noticing. Both facts come from the same
place: iodine.

**Buy it unseasoned, unsalted and un-oiled.** That is the only form this record
covers, and it rules out most of what is sold. Seasoned 김 — the glossy salted
snack sheets, the little travel packs — is a different food.

🚩 **And there is an allergen hiding in the seasoned kind: sesame.** Commercial
seasoned 김 is very often finished with sesame oil, sesame seeds, or both, and
**sesame has been one of the nine major US allergens since January 2023.** Plain
laver contains no major allergen at all; the seasoning is where one appears.
This makes 김 a label-reading item rather than a safe-by-default one — check the
ingredient list, not the front of the package.

**Now the iodine, which is the real subject of this page.** Dried nori carries
**2,316.7 µg of iodine per 100 g**, with a measured range of **1,640 to 2,850**
(USDA's iodine database, Release 4). NIH's Office of Dietary Supplements puts a
household portion on it: **2 tablespoons of flakes, about 5 g, supply 116 µg —
77% of the Daily Value.**

The Daily Value is an adult label reference. Against a baby's own number the
picture is sharper: the adequate intake for a 7-to-12-month-old is **130 µg a
day**, so ⚠️ **5 g of 김 flakes is roughly 89% of an infant's entire daily
reference intake.** That last figure is **arithmetic we did** — 116 divided by
130 — not a number any body published. Two tablespoons of flakes is not much
food.

🚨 **And here is the part that has no comfortable answer: there is no upper
intake level for iodine in infants.** NIH ODS lists the UL for both 0–6 months
and 7–12 months as **"Not possible to establish"** — the Food and Nutrition
Board judged the evidence insufficient to set one. The footnote reads that
formula and food should be the only sources of iodine for infants.

**That is an absence of a limit, not permission, and it is not a reversal of
anything.** Nobody withdrew a ceiling; one was never established. So the
question "how much 김 is too much?" genuinely has **no guideline answer**, and
this record will not manufacture one — you will find no maximum-per-week figure
here, because inventing one would be worse than admitting the gap.

What can be said honestly:

- A small amount is already a large fraction of the daily reference intake.
- It is **not a food to give freely or every day**, the way you might with
  [[broccoli]] or [[oatmeal]].
- Even the same product varies. The USDA range spans ±25% around the mean, and
  across commercial seaweeds generally ODS reports concentrations from **16 µg/g
  to 2,984 µg/g** — a 186-fold spread. **You cannot infer the dose from the
  package**, and that, rather than any number, is the actionable fact.
- Too much iodine causes some of the same problems as too little — goiter,
  elevated TSH, hypothyroidism.

If a baby is eating 김 most days, that is worth raising with your pediatrician.
That suggestion is **ours**; no guideline body says it.

For the seaweed where this gets genuinely extreme, see [[miyeok]] — and the note
there about 다시마, which is in a different league again.

## Prep 6-8

Buy plain roasted or plain dried sheets. Check the ingredients: it should say
laver or seaweed, and nothing else. No salt, no oil, no sesame.

**Crumble it over food** rather than handing over a sheet — over rice, over
[[oatmeal]], over mashed vegetables. A pinch is a serving at this age.

Two reasons for crumbling rather than handing over. The practical one: a dry
sheet of 김 goes limp and sticks the instant it touches anything wet, including
a wet hand. The cautious one: a dry sheet can adhere to the roof of the mouth.
**No guideline body names that as a hazard** — it is ordinary caution, not an
issued rule, and it is the reason this food is rated low-risk rather than
modify-required.

Do not use 김 as the vehicle for rice or as a wrapper at this age.

## Prep 9-11

Still crumbled, still plain, still small amounts. A pincer grasp makes flakes
easy to pick up off a tray directly, which some babies enjoy more than having it
mixed in.

Frequency, not portion, is the thing to keep an eye on now — it is an easy food
to reach for daily because it is convenient and babies like it.

## Prep 12-17

A toddler can manage torn pieces of a plain sheet, and 김 with rice is a normal
thing to eat. The constraints that stay are the two this page is about: **plain,
and not every day.**

Seasoned 김 is what most households actually have in the cupboard. It carries
salt, oil, and often sesame. If you serve it, treat it as a salted snack food
rather than a vegetable, and check the label for sesame if that allergen matters
in your family.

## Safety

**Iodine is the governing issue, and it has no numeric ceiling to give you.** No
UL exists for infants — "not possible to establish" — so there is no
guideline-issued maximum, and this record declines to invent one. Practically:
small amounts, not daily, and raise it with your pediatrician if 김 has become
an everyday food. The last part is our inference.

**Content is unpredictable per package.** A 186-fold range across commercial
seaweeds, and ±25% within dried nori alone. No amount of label-reading tells you
the dose.

🚩 **Sesame in seasoned 김.** Plain laver has no major allergen; the seasoned
product frequently contains sesame seeds or sesame oil, and sesame is one of the
nine. Read the ingredient list.

**Sodium in seasoned 김.** Salted snack sheets are salty by design. Sodium
adequate intake is 110 mg a day from birth to six months and 370 mg from seven
to twelve months (NASEM 2019) — note that these are adequacy targets, not
ceilings, since no UL or EAR for sodium exists at any age.

**Choking risk is low.** The dry-sheet-sticking-to-the-palate caution above is
ordinary practice, not a recognised mechanism named by any body.

**No guideline body addresses 김 as an infant food.** The iodine figures, the
absence of an infant UL and sesame's allergen status are all issued or measured.
The serving advice — crumble it, keep it small, do not make it daily — is our
inference from those facts.
