## Background

A plain boiled or baked potato is one of the most useful ordinary foods on this
list. It cooks to exactly the texture CDC asks for, it is cheap, and babies
generally like it.

**Its job is vitamin C, not iron.** Boiled potato carries 0.31 mg of iron per
100 g, which is negligible, and **13.0 mg of vitamin C**, which is not. Vitamin C
eaten in the same meal improves absorption of plant and fortified (non-heme)
iron, so potato earns its place beside the foods actually carrying iron —
[[lentils]], [[black-beans]], [[fortified-infant-cereal]], [[teff]]. A bowl of
mashed lentils with potato is doing real work; a bowl of potato alone is
carbohydrate.

The safety note below is the part worth reading properly. It is not about
choking — a cooked potato is soft — it is about glycoalkaloids, where the usual
household habit of cutting the green bit off is the wrong response.

## Prep 6-8

**Peel it.** The glycoalkaloids that matter concentrate in the peel, and peel
does not break down under gums in any case.

Boil, steam or bake until a piece gives way completely under light pressure
between two fingers, then cut into finger-length wedges about as thick as two of
your fingers. At this age the whole-hand grasp means the piece is held in the
fist with an end sticking out, so the baby controls the bite. Small pieces are
harder now, not safer.

Mash is equally good, thinned with breast milk, formula, or the cooking water.
Skip butter and salt.

## Prep 9-11

Soft cubes about thumbnail size once the pincer grasp arrives, with a few larger
pieces alongside so biting and tearing still get practised. Potato takes other
foods well — mashed [[black-beans]], flaked [[salmon]], puréed [[broccoli]].

## Prep 12-17

However the household eats it, kept soft and unsalted. Fried potatoes are a
different food: hard-edged, oily and salted, and none of those helps here.
Gamjajeon, gamjatang and other salted dishes are the family's food, and a baby's
portion is better taken out before the salt goes in.

## Safety

**Discard green or sprouted potatoes. Do not trim them and use the rest.** This
is the whole safety note, and it runs against normal kitchen practice.

Potato glycoalkaloids — solanine and its relatives — concentrate in the sprouts
and flowers at **200 to 500 mg per 100 g**, and in the peel of bitter varieties
at **150 to 220 mg per 100 g**, against under 10 mg per 100 g in a healthy
tuber. Levels build with light exposure, which is what greening is. They inhibit
cholinesterase and, at dose, cause haemorrhagic gut damage.

**Cooking does not fix it.** FAO is explicit: glycoalkaloids "are not destroyed
during normal cooking because the decomposition temperature of solanine is about
243 °C." Nothing that happens in a domestic kitchen reaches that.

**And taste is not a guard.** Bitterness only becomes detectable at around **20
mg per 100 g — roughly twice the level of a normal healthy tuber.** So a potato
carrying a meaningfully elevated dose can taste perfectly fine, and a baby who
cannot tell you it tastes odd is the worst possible taster for it.

Put together: peel every potato, store them dark, and throw out anything green,
sprouting or soft. A potato costs less than the alternative.

**No infant-specific safe threshold has been established** in anything this
record could verify, so no number is given here for what is acceptable in a
baby's portion. The rule is avoidance of the affected tuber, not a limit.
