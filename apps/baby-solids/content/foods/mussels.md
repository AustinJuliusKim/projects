## Background

Mussels are ordinary table food in a lot of households, and there is no reason a
six-month-old can't eat them once they are cooked properly and taken apart
carefully. What this record cannot give you is the nutrition number you were
probably expecting.

**The composition figures are not verified and are deliberately left out.**
Mussels are conventionally described as an iron-rich food, and that may well be
true, but the USDA database was unavailable when this record was researched and
NIH's iron fact sheet lists only oysters among the shellfish. Rather than print
a number we could not check, this record prints none. The iron in shellfish
flesh is the heme form, the kind CDC says is absorbed more readily than the
non-heme iron in plants — that is a classification, not a measurement, and it is
all we are willing to state. If you are serving mussels *for* the iron, [[beef]]
and [[oyster]] both carry figures we could verify.

**On allergy, mussels are not what most families assume.** Mussels are molluscs.
Molluscs are **not** among the nine major food allergens in US law — that list
is milk, egg, fish, **crustacean** shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans
and sesame, and FDA glosses crustacean as crab, lobster and shrimp. Oyster,
mussel, clam and scallop appear on none of it. In January 2025 FDA issued final
guidance setting out how it evaluates allergens *other* than the nine, which is
the category molluscs fall into.

Two practical consequences, and they cut in opposite directions. A mollusc
allergy is real and can be severe, but **a US label is not required to warn you
about it** — "Contains: shellfish" means crustacean. The label shortcut that
works for peanut or milk does not work here, so the ingredient list has to be
read. Conversely, a child who reacted to shrimp has not necessarily reacted to
mussels; they are different animals. This record does not print a
cross-reactivity percentage because no source we could verify gives one for
infants.

## Prep 6-8

Buy them live and cook them the same day. Before cooking, tap any mussel whose
shell is open — CDC's instruction is to **discard shellfish that are already
open before cooking**, and that is the stricter of the two rules in circulation,
so it is the one this record publishes.

Cook by boiling until the shells open, then **keep boiling another 3 to 5
minutes**. Steaming works too, 4 to 9 minutes. **Throw away any that do not open
fully.** FDA stops at "the shells open during cooking — throw out ones that
don't open"; CDC asks for the extra minutes past that point. For a baby, take
the longer rule.

Then take the meat apart properly, which is where the actual work is:

- **Pull the beard.** The byssal threads are the tough fibrous tuft on the flat
  side. They do not soften with cooking and they are not something a baby can
  break down.
- **Feel for shell chips.** Shells crack during cooking and fragments end up in
  the meat. Run each mussel between your fingers before it goes on the tray.
- **Cut to no larger than half an inch** — AAP's dimension, and the only
  dimensional rule any US guideline body actually issues.

Chop the cleaned meat fine and stir in a spoonful of the cooking liquid or a
puree the baby already eats so it holds together in a fist.

## Prep 9-11

Same cleaning, same cooking, slightly larger pieces. A pincer grasp arrives for
most babies around nine to ten months and makes half-inch pieces easy to pick up
where they used to be pushed around the tray. The beard, the shell check and the
half-inch limit do not change.

## Prep 12-17

Mussels as the household eats them, still shucked, still bearded, still
checked by hand, still cut down. Serve them out of the pot before wine, stock
cubes or salt go in — a seasoned adult portion of shellfish broth carries far
more sodium than a toddler's day should.

Never the shell as a toy or a scoop. A cracked shell edge is sharp.

## Safety

**Mercury: we do not know, and that is the honest answer.** Mussels appear on
**no** FDA/EPA mercury list — not Best Choices, not Good Choices, not the "avoid"
list. That is an **absence of assessment, not a clearance.** The advice does not
say mussels are fine; it does not consider them at all. Treat them as one food
among several rather than a weekly fixture, and if you want an actual position,
ask your pediatrician rather than reading reassurance into a gap.

**Raw is out, at every age.** FDA names children among the groups who should
avoid raw or undercooked fish or shellfish. That applies to mussels served
barely opened as much as to raw oysters.

**The beard and the shell chips are the choking and injury story**, not the
meat itself, which is soft once cooked. Both are removal jobs, and both are easy
to skip when you are cooking a pot of mussels for adults and pulling a portion
out for the baby. Do the baby's portion separately and deliberately.

**No guideline body addresses mussels as an infant food.** Everything specific
above — the beard, the shell check, chopping fine — is a category rule for
shellfish safety and choking applied to this food by us, plus ordinary practice.
The cooking times and the half-inch limit are the parts that are actually
issued.
