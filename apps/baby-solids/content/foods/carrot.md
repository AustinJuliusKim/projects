## Background

Carrot is the most heavily flagged food in this batch, and the reason is narrow:
**raw** carrot is named by name on both US hazard lists — CDC's, which lists
"raw carrots" among hard raw vegetables, and AAP's, which names "raw vegetables
like carrot sticks" among foods to avoid until age four or older. Two bodies
naming the same food is rare.

Cooked carrot is a different food mechanically and is fine. CDC's instruction is
explicit: "Cook hard vegetables and fruits, such as carrots and apples, so you
can easily mash or puree them." That single sentence is the whole record.

Nutritionally it is unremarkable here — 0.34 mg of non-heme iron per 100 g, and
3.6 mg of vitamin C, which is too little to count as the vitamin-C partner for
an iron food. CDC does not list carrots among its vitamin-C foods; use
[[broccoli]] for that job. Serve carrot because it is sweet, cheap, holds its
shape when cooked, and babies like it.

## Prep 6-8

Peel, cut into batons roughly the length of an adult finger, and steam, boil, or
roast until a fork goes through with no resistance and the piece mashes when you
press it. Undercooked carrot is the failure mode — it looks done, softens on the
outside, and stays firm in the middle. Test the thickest piece, not the thinnest.

That baton length is common practice, not a rule any body issues. AAP's one
published dimension is to cut food for infants into pieces no larger than half an
inch, and everything else about shape and length in circulation — here and
everywhere — is convention. What actually carries the safety is the mash test.

## Prep 9-11

Soft cubes, picked up between finger and thumb. Keep a few longer soft batons on
the tray as well, since biting a piece off is its own skill.

## Prep 12-17

However the family eats cooked carrot — in soups, roasted, mashed, stirred
through rice. Raw carrot is still out; see below.

## Safety

**Raw carrot is the hazard, and it does not stop being one at twelve months.**
AAP's list of foods to avoid — raw carrot sticks among them — runs to "4 years of
age or older." The concern is a hard food that does not compress meeting an
airway that is still small.

This record allows carrot with a required modification rather than banning it,
because the cooking *is* the modification. If it does not mash under a fork, it
has not been modified yet, and it is still the raw-carrot problem in a warm
disguise.

One place the bodies differ: the Canadian Paediatric Society offers **grating**
as a way to serve raw vegetables, where AAP's list simply keeps raw carrot out
until four. Grated raw carrot is not the same object as a stick — but it is
still hard, and AAP names no exception for it. This app follows the more
cautious of the two and treats cooking as the modification for carrot in this
age range.

**On carrot coins, an honest note.** You will be told never to serve cooked
carrot in rounds. The reasoning is sound — CDC's rule for round foods is to cut
them into small pieces or short thin strips rather than discs, because a disc
can seal an airway. But that rule is written about grapes, cherries, berries,
tomatoes, hot dogs, and string cheese. **Carrot is not named in it.** Cutting
cooked carrot into sticks or half-moons rather than coins is a sensible
extrapolation from a guideline, not an instruction from one — and if the carrot
is cooked soft enough to mash, the shape is much less of the story than the
texture is.
