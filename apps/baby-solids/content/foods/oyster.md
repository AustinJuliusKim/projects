## Background

### 🚨 Never raw. Not once, not a taste, not even a "treated" one.

This belongs at the top because raw oysters are a normal adult food and will be
in the house — on a platter at a party, in a box from the fish counter, at a
grandparent's table. **FDA names children among the at-risk groups who should
avoid "raw or undercooked fish or shellfish" and, specifically, "raw oysters,
even if they are treated after they have been harvested."** Post-harvest
treatment kills some pathogens; FDA says plainly it does not remove all of them.

CDC is blunter about why you cannot manage this by judgement:

- **"You cannot tell that an oyster is bad by looking at it."** There is no
  smell, no colour, no texture that sorts the safe one from the dangerous one.
- You can get sick from oysters in **any month of the year** — the "months with
  an R" rule is not a safety rule.
- **Hot sauce and lemon juice do not kill *Vibrio*.** Neither does alcohol.
- For *Vibrio vulnificus*, **about 1 in 5 people with the infection die**,
  sometimes within a day or two.

There is no age at which raw oyster becomes appropriate in this app's range, and
no preparation short of cooking that fixes it. Cooked properly, an oyster is an
excellent food for a baby. That is the whole distinction.

---

**Now the good part, because it is genuinely remarkable.** NIH's Office of
Dietary Supplements says it outright: **"Oysters contain more zinc per serving
than any other food."** Not "one of the best" — the most. Three ounces of cooked
Pacific oysters carry **28.2 mg of zinc**; three ounces of raw farmed eastern
oysters, 32 mg. The zinc RDA for a 7-to-12-month-old is **3 mg a day.** A portion
far smaller than an adult serving covers it several times over, which is exactly
why this food does not need volume to be worth serving.

Zinc matters at this age for the same reason iron does: it is one of the two
nutrients that make the old "food before one is just for fun" line false. Breast
milk alone stops covering both somewhere around six months.

Oyster is also a real iron food, and one of the few where we have a figure we
could verify: **3 oz of cooked oysters supply 8 mg of iron, 44% of the Daily
Value**, against an 11 mg/day RDA for a 7-to-12-month-old. It is **heme** iron —
the form CDC says is absorbed more readily than the non-heme iron in plants, and
which, unlike the iron in [[lentils]] or [[spinach]], does not depend on a
vitamin-C food alongside it.

This record deliberately does **not** print an iron-per-100-g figure. The
verified number is per three-ounce serving; converting it to a per-100-g value
would be our arithmetic rather than a measured value, and the USDA database was
unavailable when this was researched. The per-serving figure above is the one to
trust.

**Iodine, worth knowing about but not alarming here.** Cooked oysters carry
about 109 µg per 100 g in USDA's iodine database, with a measured range of 81 to
132. A 100 g portion would be a large fraction of the 130 µg daily adequate
intake for a 7-to-12-month-old — but a baby's actual portion is a fraction of
that, and oyster's iodine is nowhere near the scale of the seaweeds. If you want
the food where iodine genuinely governs the serving size, that is [[gim]] and
[[miyeok]], not this.

**On allergy: oysters are molluscs, and molluscs are not among the nine US major
allergens.** The nine are milk, egg, fish, **crustacean** shellfish, tree nuts,
peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame; FDA glosses crustacean as crab, lobster and
shrimp. FDA's January 2025 final guidance covers how it evaluates allergens
outside that list, which is where molluscs sit. The practical upshot: mollusc
allergy is real and can be severe, but **a US label need not declare it**, so
you have to read the ingredient list rather than trust a "Contains" line. And a
child who reacted to shrimp has not necessarily reacted to oyster.

## Prep 6-8

Buy them from a source you trust, refrigerated, and cook them the day you buy
them. If they are in the shell, discard any that are already open **before**
cooking.

**Cooking in the shell:** boil until the shells open, then continue boiling
**another 3 to 5 minutes.** Or steam 4 to 9 minutes. Discard any that do not
open fully. FDA's shorter rule stops at "the shells open"; CDC asks for the
extra minutes, and that is the rule this record publishes.

**Shucked oysters** — the more likely form for cooking — have their own set:
boil for at least 3 minutes, or fry at 375 °F for at least 3 minutes, or broil
3 minutes, or bake at 450 °F for 10 minutes.

Then shuck (if not already), feel through the meat for shell chips, chop fine,
and moisten with a little of the cooking liquid so it gathers rather than
scatters. Keep the portion small — this is a food where a teaspoon is doing
serious nutritional work.

Serve it plain. Oyster sauces, jarred oysters in brine and anything
restaurant-seasoned carry sodium well beyond a baby's day.

## Prep 9-11

The same cooking rules, in pieces up to half an inch that a pincer grasp can
manage. Stirred through [[oatmeal]], soft rice or mashed vegetables it goes down
more easily than on its own, and it carries a strong flavour that some babies
need a few tries to accept.

## Prep 12-17

However the family eats them — **cooked**. The raw prohibition does not lift at
twelve months, or at any age in this app's range. Watch the seasoning rather
than the oyster: take the child's share out before soy sauce, gochujang or salt
goes in.

## Safety

**Raw oyster is the hazard, and it is absolute.** See the callout above. The
short version: FDA says children should avoid raw oysters even when
post-harvest-treated; CDC says you cannot identify a bad one by looking, that
any month is a risk, that hot sauce and lemon do nothing, and that about one in
five people infected with *Vibrio vulnificus* die. Cook them.

**Shell chips** are the choking-and-injury risk once cooked. Feel through the
meat with your fingers before it goes on the tray.

**Size.** No piece larger than half an inch — AAP's rule, and the only
dimensional rule any US guideline body issues.

**Mercury is not a concern here.** FDA and EPA place oysters in **Best Choices**,
and within that on the sub-list of choices that are *even lower* in mercury.

**Portion, not frequency, is the lever.** No body publishes a serving size for
shellfish under twelve months, so this record gives none. What it can say is
that the zinc and iron content means a small portion is already substantial, and
that a food this nutrient-dense does not need to be served in adult quantities
to count.

**No guideline body addresses oyster as an infant food specifically.** The raw
prohibition, the cooking times, the mercury rating and the half-inch limit are
all issued. Chopping fine, checking for shell chips and keeping the portion
small are category rules and ordinary practice applied by us.
