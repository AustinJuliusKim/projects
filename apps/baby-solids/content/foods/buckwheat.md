## Background

Buckwheat is not wheat and not a cereal grain at all — it is a seed, botanically
closer to rhubarb. Cooked groats carry 0.80 mg of iron per 100 g, which is
modest; the dry roasted groats measure 2.47 mg per 100 g, but that is before the
water goes in. It earns its place here on flavour and texture and on being a
familiar Korean and Japanese food, not on iron.

**The allergen situation is genuinely asymmetric between the countries in this
family's life, and both directions of confusion are common.**

- **In the United States, buckwheat is not one of the nine major food
  allergens.** No US label has to declare it, and none of the FDA labelling
  rules apply to it.
- **Japan and Korea both require buckwheat to be declared on food labels.**
  Korea's list goes beyond the common eight to include buckwheat, beef, chicken,
  peach, pork, tomato and pine nut, reflecting what actually triggers
  immediate-type reactions in Korean children. Korea is also the only country
  where precautionary "may contain" labelling is mandated by law.

So the same product, sold in Seoul and in Los Angeles, carries a buckwheat
declaration in one place and legally need not in the other. **A parent who
learns "buckwheat isn't an allergen" from a US source will misread a Korean
label as alarming; a parent who learns it from Korean relatives will hunt for a
US warning that does not have to exist.** Neither country is wrong. The
asymmetry is the fact, and knowing it is what keeps a label from being
misread in either direction.

(Allergen lists are revised periodically — Japan's has changed more than once in
the last few years — so treat any count you read, including in older reviews, as
a snapshot rather than a constant.)

**"Buckwheat is gluten-free" is true of the seed and false of most soba.**
Buckwheat itself contains no gluten. Commercial soba noodles are commonly
blended with wheat flour, sometimes mostly wheat flour, and wheat *is* one of
the US nine. If wheat matters in your household, the label is the only thing
that settles it — the word "soba" does not.

## Prep 6-8

Simmer groats until they are soft all the way through, well past the point where
they still have a bite, then mash or grind them. **A cooked buckwheat groat is a
cooked whole grain kernel**, and CDC's instruction for those is to cook and then
finely grind or mash — buckwheat is not named, but "and other grains" is, and
this is one.

Mashed groats fold into puréed [[butternut-squash]] or [[apple]] easily. Buckwheat
carries no vitamin C, so if you want the iron to count, put a vitamin-C food in
the same bowl.

Soba is not a 6–8 month food in any useful form, and most of it contains wheat
in any case.

## Prep 9-11

Softly cooked groats, mashed rather than ground, mixed through other foods.
Buckwheat porridge takes strong flavours well, which is useful at the age when
plain food starts getting refused.

If you introduce soba here, cut it into short lengths a baby can bite through
rather than serving long strands. No guideline body addresses noodle length, so
that is judgement rather than instruction.

## Prep 12-17

However the table eats it, kept soft. Memil-guksu and soba are normal family
food at this age; cut noodles short, skip the dipping sauce, and check the
package for wheat if that matters to you. Cold noodle broths are usually heavily
salted, which is a separate reason to serve the noodles plain.

## Safety

**Cooked groats are whole grain kernels** and sit inside CDC's grinding rule.
Cook them soft and mash or grind them; the degree is not specified by anyone, so
judge it by whether a kernel still has structure between two fingers.

**On allergy, hold both facts at once.** Buckwheat is not a US major allergen
and carries no US labelling requirement. It is a recognised, labelled allergen
in Japan and Korea, which is a real signal about how often it causes reactions
in children there — not a labelling quirk. Introduce it the way you would any
new food, watch the first exposure as you would any first exposure, and read
Korean and Japanese labels knowing what they are telling you.

**Soba is a wheat question until the label says otherwise.**
