## Background

Spinach has a better pedigree as a first food than its reputation suggests. NHS
names it among the vegetables to *start* weaning with — "vegetables that aren't
so sweet, such as broccoli, cauliflower and spinach" — and CDC's 6-to-12-month
list leads with cooked spinach. Two guideline bodies naming a single vegetable
by name is unusual.

The iron figure in this record, 1.26 mg per 100 g, is for **raw baby spinach**
(mature raw is 1.05). A USDA entry for cooked spinach could not be verified at
the time of writing, so none is quoted — cooking wilts spinach dramatically, so
the same weight of cooked spinach represents far more leaf than the same weight
raw, and the two numbers are not interchangeable.

**The oxalate story is wrong, and worth correcting.** You will be told that
spinach iron "doesn't count" because oxalate binds it. The absorption part is
true; the explanation is not.

Bonsmann and colleagues ran a randomized crossover trial using stable iron
isotopes, measuring incorporation into red cells at fourteen days. A kale meal
gave 10.7% iron absorption. The same kale meal with 1.26 g of potassium oxalate
added gave 11.5% — **P = 0.86, no difference at all.** The spinach meal ran
about 24% below kale, but not significantly. The authors' conclusion is that
potassium oxalate did not influence iron absorption in humans, and that oxalic
acid in fruits and vegetables is of minor relevance to iron nutrition. They
attribute spinach's disadvantage to **calcium and polyphenols** instead.

So: spinach is a modest non-heme iron food that is poorly absorbed, for reasons
that are real but are not oxalate. Oxalate's established relevance is calcium
binding and kidney stones, not infant iron status.

The practical advice does not change much, and it is the same advice as for
every plant iron source: pair it with a vitamin-C food ([[mango]],
[[green-peas]], [[butternut-squash]]) and do not pair it with dairy — the
calcium in [[yogurt]] works against you in the same meal.

**Nitrates: the US and the EU genuinely disagree.** Both positions are given
here because both are defensible and they lead to slightly different habits.

**The US position is permissive — and has quietly gone quiet.** AAP's 2005
clinical report on infant methemoglobinemia identified **well water** as the
real high-risk vector, found "little or no risk" from commercially prepared US
infant foods, and noted that reports of nitrate poisoning from **home-prepared**
vegetable foods continued to occur. **AAP retired that report in June 2025 and
has not replaced it**, so the US no longer has a current statement on dietary
nitrate in infancy at all. What remains is that CDC and the Dietary Guidelines
impose no nitrate restriction whatsoever, CDC actively recommends cooked spinach
for 6-to-12-month-olds, and NHS recommends it as a first vegetable.

The retirement is worth knowing precisely because of what it is *not*: there is
no new US finding that nitrate is more dangerous than previously thought. A
retired statement is an absence of guidance, not a reversal of it.

**The EU position is more cautious.** EFSA's CONTAM Panel set a nitrate ADI of
3.7 mg per kg of body weight per day and concluded that current nitrate levels
in vegetables are "not of health concern for most children" — but added that a
risk of methaemoglobinaemia cannot be excluded for 1-to-3-year-olds eating high
amounts, and that for infants a risk "cannot be excluded" for those eating more
than one spinach meal in a day. EFSA also recommends that children with
bacterial gastrointestinal infections not be given spinach, because infection
increases the conversion of nitrate to nitrite. Germany's BfR adds that cooked
spinach should not be reheated.

**What to actually do.** The US is not wrong that spinach is a good first food —
it plainly is, and two guideline bodies recommend it by name. But the EU's three
precautions cost nothing and the US does not contradict any of them, so this
record treats them as the default:

- No more than one spinach meal in a day.
- Do not reheat cooked spinach — cook it fresh, or serve leftovers cold.
- Skip it while a child has a gastrointestinal infection.

If your water comes from a private well, that is the part worth acting on: test
it for nitrate, and use tested water for anything you make up for an infant.
That was the strongest point in AAP's retired report, and nothing has come along
to weaken it.

## Prep 6-8

Wash it well — spinach carries grit, and grit is the most common actual problem
with it. Wilt it in a pan with a splash of water for two or three minutes until
it collapses, then squeeze out the liquid and **chop it finely**.

Then mix it into something with body: mashed [[butternut-squash]], [[barley]]
cereal, [[tofu]], mashed beans. A pile of loose cooked spinach is hard to pick
up and easy to gather into a wad; carried in a thicker food it goes down easily
and tastes less sharp.

Cook it fresh rather than reheating a batch. Cool leftovers quickly, refrigerate
them, and serve them cold or not at all.

## Prep 9-11

Same handling — cooked, squeezed, finely chopped, carried in something. The
pincer grasp does not help much with a leaf; what changes is that the baby can
now self-feed a thick spinach-and-grain mixture from a loaded spoon or as small
soft lumps.

A cooked spinach and egg or tofu scramble holds together well enough to pick up,
which is the first form of spinach most babies manage on their own.

## Prep 12-17

However the household eats it, still cooked and chopped rather than served as
whole leaves. Stirred into rice or pasta, in a soup, in sigeumchi namul made
without salt or soy sauce, in a dal.

Raw spinach salad is a different proposition — thin whole leaves are the wad
problem in its purest form — and is better left until well past this range.

## Safety

**Texture, not hardness.** Spinach is soft, so it is easy to assume it needs no
modification. The hazard is that thin cooked leaves stick together and to the
mouth, and a mouthful of whole leaves can gather into a slippery wad. Chopping
finely and mixing into a carrier food solves it.

**The three EU precautions** above are the practical nitrate advice: one spinach
meal a day at most, never reheated, skipped during a gastrointestinal infection.

**Home-made purée storage.** AAP's report singles out home-prepared vegetable
foods as the place where infant nitrate poisoning still occurs. Cool it fast,
refrigerate it promptly, and do not keep it for days — the same handling that
[[butternut-squash]] and other home-made vegetable purées need.

**Well water.** If your household is on a private well, have it tested for
nitrate. That is the vector AAP considers genuinely high-risk, and it has
nothing to do with which vegetables you serve.
