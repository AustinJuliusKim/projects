## Background

Rye grain carries 2.63 mg of iron per 100 g, which is respectable for a home-
cooked whole grain — better than [[quinoa]], better than [[teff]]. Cooked rye
berries, ground, are the form that belongs in an infant's bowl. The bread is a
different matter, and the safety note below is about it.

**Rye is a gluten grain. It is not wheat, and it is not one of the nine major US
allergens.** FDA's definition of gluten names "wheat, barley, and rye" together,
so a food containing rye cannot be labelled gluten-free. But rye carries no US
allergen-labelling requirement, and **a child with an IgE wheat allergy is not
automatically reactive to rye.**

Do not read that as permission, though, because the converse trips people up:
**most commercial rye bread contains wheat flour** — often more wheat than rye.
Rye is therefore **not a substitute for wheat in a wheat-allergic child**, both
because the product usually contains wheat anyway and because that decision
belongs with the clinician managing the allergy, not with a labelling
technicality. And rye is unsafe in celiac disease, full stop.

**On timing, there is nothing to wait for and nothing to optimise.** ESPGHAN's
2024 update — a reaffirming replacement of its 2016 position, not a retirement —
holds that gluten introduced at any time between completed 4 months and 12
months does not affect the cumulative incidence of celiac disease. It also says
this, which is worth quoting exactly rather than paraphrasing:

> "There is no specific guidance on the type of gluten-containing foods to be
> introduced at weaning."

**ESPGHAN is explicitly declining to rank the gluten grains.** Anyone who tells
you rye is a better or worse first gluten grain than wheat or [[barley]] is
giving you an opinion. So is anyone who ranks them the other way. There is no
guidance here, and this record is not going to invent one.

## Prep 6-8

Whole rye berries need a long simmer — an hour or more, well past the chewy
texture an adult would stop at — and then grinding or mashing. **A cooked rye
berry is a whole grain kernel**, and CDC's instruction for those is to cook and
then finely grind or mash. Rye is not named in the list; "and other grains" is.

Ground cooked rye stirs into puréed vegetables or fortified cereal. Pair it with
a vitamin-C food, since the iron is non-heme: [[potato]], [[broccoli]].

Rye flour porridge is a simpler route to the same place, and cooked thin it
needs no grinding.

## Prep 9-11

Softly cooked, mashed rye berries mixed through other foods, or rye porridge
thickened up. Rye is high in fibre and strongly flavoured — start small alongside
something familiar rather than as a bowl on its own.

## Prep 12-17

Rye as part of family meals, kept soft and mashed where it stayed firm. **Rye
bread is still not appropriate here** — the sodium is the reason, and it does not
change at twelve months.

## Safety

**Rye bread is a hard exclusion under 24 months, whatever its iron says.** USDA
measures rye bread at **603 mg of sodium per 100 g**. Its iron looks appealing at
2.83 mg per 100 g, and that is the trap: the sodium arrives in the same slice.
CDC's advice is to avoid added salt in this age group, and a food carrying six
hundred milligrams per hundred grams is not an infant food. Dense rye breads and
crispbreads are also firm, and crispbreads with whole kernels or seeds are
separately on CDC's hazard list.

**Cooked rye berries are whole grain kernels** — cook them very soft, then grind
or mash.

**Gluten, not wheat allergy — keep the two apart.** Rye belongs in a conversation
about celiac disease, where it is unsafe. It does not belong in a wheat-free
diet as a substitute, because most rye products contain wheat and because that
call is a clinician's.
