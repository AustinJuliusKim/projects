## Background

Duck leg is the surprise of the poultry group. Braised skinless duck meat carries
2.33 mg of iron per 100 g and roasted duck with skin 2.08 mg, against
[[chicken-thigh]]'s 1.13 mg — roughly 1.8 to 2.1 times the iron for a bird most
people think of as a special occasion. It is heme iron, the readily absorbed
form, which puts duck leg in the same league as [[beef]] rather than in
chicken's.

**We publish no zinc figure for duck leg.** USDA's records for both the braised
and the roasted leg simply do not carry one. There is a zinc value on USDA's
all-parts duck record, but that is a different food — a composite of the whole
carcass, not the leg — and borrowing it would be inventing a number for this
page. If you want duck for zinc, [[lamb]] and [[turkey-dark-meat]] have verified
figures and duck does not.

Sodium in plain braised duck meat is 108 mg per 100 g, which is ordinary for
fresh meat. Everything in the Safety section below is about preparations where
that stops being true.

Duck is not one of the nine major US allergens.

## Prep 6-8

Skin and bone come off **before** cooking, per CDC. With duck that is also the
practical move: the skin carries most of the fat, and a duck leg cooked with its
skin on renders enough fat to make the meat greasy for a baby.

Braise the skinned leg in water or unsalted stock until the meat leaves the bone
without effort — usually an hour and a half to two hours. Let the cooking liquid
settle, spoon the fat off the top, then mince the meat fine and moisten it with
the defatted liquid.

Cook duck pieces to 165 °F (USDA FSIS), the same as any other poultry. A braise
passes that long before the meat is soft enough to serve.

## Prep 9-11

Shredded. Braised duck leg falls into long soft strands that suit a pincer grasp
well. Stirred through [[oatmeal]], rice, or a mashed vegetable it is less greasy
on the hands than duck served alone.

## Prep 12-17

However the family eats it, still deboned, still soft, still cut small — and
still not any of the cured or glazed preparations below. That rules out most of
the ways duck actually appears on a table, which is the honest situation rather
than an oversight.

## Safety

**Colour is not a doneness test, and duck is the food where that bites.** USDA
FSIS says plainly that duck cooked to 165 °F **may still be pink in the centre**.
Duck is also the bird most often served deliberately rare, so both errors are
live here: a cook who trusts pinkness will keep cooking meat that is already
safe, and a cook who has been told duck is "meant to be pink" may serve a baby
meat that never got there. Use a thermometer. FDA is explicit that children
should only eat poultry cooked to a safe internal temperature, so rare duck
breast — and any duck served pink on purpose — is out for a baby regardless of
how it looks.

**Confit is excluded.** Duck confit is salt-cured before it is cooked in fat.
That is the point of the technique, and it puts confit in the same category as
other cured meats under CDC's advice against high-sodium foods for children
under two. We publish no sodium figure for it: none was verified against a
primary source while this record was written.

**Peking duck and Chinese roast duck are excluded**, for added sugar as well as
sodium — the glaze and the marinade are both. CDC advises against added sugars
under two years as well as high sodium. Again, no figure is published here
because none was verified.

**Bones and chunks.** Duck legs are small and their bones splinter more readily
than chicken's. Debone before cooking, check the shredded meat by hand, and keep
the meat finely chopped or shredded rather than cubed — chunks of meat stay on
CDC's hazard list past the first birthday.
