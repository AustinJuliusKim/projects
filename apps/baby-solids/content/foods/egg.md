## Background

Egg is the second-best-evidenced allergen introduction after peanut, and the
evidence carries an unusual condition attached: **the cooking is part of the
intervention.** A 2018 review and meta-analysis found moderate-certainty
evidence that introducing egg between four and six months reduces egg allergy,
with "much better efficacy with using cooked as opposed to raw egg." AAP's
wording is the same direction: well-cooked eggs have been studied and shown to
reduce the risk of egg allergy when fed to infants regularly.

So there is no tension between the allergy advice and the food-safety advice
here — well-cooked egg is both the safe form and the effective one.

Two things from AAP are worth carrying: **about 1/3 of a well-cooked egg** as
the portion, and **at least weekly** as the cadence. Introduction is not the
finish line for allergens; a single taste followed by months of nothing is not
what the trials tested.

Egg is also on CDC's list of heme iron foods, at 1.19 mg per 100 g. That is the
classification, given here as CDC gives it.

Related: [[salmon]] is the other major allergen in this batch and follows the
same medical gate.

## Prep 6-8

Scramble in a little unsalted butter or oil over low heat and keep going until
there is no wet or glossy egg left — AAP names scrambled egg among suitable
finger foods, and soft moist curds are easy to gum. Hard-boiled works too; mash
the yolk and white together with a little breast milk, formula, or water, since
a dry hard-boiled white is rubbery on its own.

Serve the whole egg rather than the yolk alone — AAP's portion is about 1/3 of
a well-cooked egg, not a third of a yolk, and a yolk-only serving is a smaller
exposure than the one that was studied.

Offer it in the morning, at home, on a day you are not rushing — that is a
logistics choice, not a medical one: it means you are watching and your clinic
is open. IgE reactions happen within minutes to two hours. That two-hour window
is also why the familiar "wait three to five days before the next new food" rule
adds nothing diagnostically; it just slows down variety.

## Prep 9-11

Same well-cooked forms in pincer-sized pieces: a thin omelette cut into small
squares, chopped hard-boiled egg, egg stirred into [[oatmeal]]. Keep it in the
rotation at least weekly rather than treating the first serving as done.

## Prep 12-17

However the family eats eggs, with one exception that does not expire at twelve
months: still cooked through. Runny yolks, soft-set custards, homemade
mayonnaise, raw batter, and mousse are all uncooked-egg foods.

## Safety

**Cook it fully.** FDA and CDC advise against raw or undercooked egg for
everyone, and children under five are singled out: they are about three times
more likely to be hospitalized with *Salmonella*. Cook eggs until the white and yolk
are firm; egg-containing dishes such as casseroles and custards go to 160 °F
(USDA FSIS).

**The bodies disagree, and you should know it.** NHS permits raw or lightly
cooked hen's eggs for babies *if* the eggs carry the British Lion stamp, a
UK-specific *Salmonella*-control scheme with no US equivalent. FDA and CDC make
no such carve-out. This app follows FDA and CDC: in the US there is no stamp
that makes a runny egg a safe infant food. If you are reading UK guidance
alongside this, that is where the difference comes from — not a disagreement
about babies, but a difference in national egg supply controls.

Choking risk from well-cooked egg is low. A whole hard-boiled egg, or a large
piece of firm white, is a different matter — mash or cut it.
