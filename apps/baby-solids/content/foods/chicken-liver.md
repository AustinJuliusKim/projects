## Background

Chicken liver has the highest iron of any food in this entire canon: **11.6 mg
per 100 g simmered, 12.9 mg pan-fried**. That is about twice [[beef-liver]] and
roughly five times [[beef]] mince, in heme form — the readily absorbed kind CDC
contrasts with the non-heme iron in plants and fortified cereals. Nothing else
here is close.

**And as with any liver, iron is not the constraint. Vitamin A is.**

Chicken liver carries 3,980 µg RAE of preformed vitamin A per 100 g simmered and
4,300 µg pan-fried. NIH ODS sets the Tolerable Upper Intake Level for preformed
vitamin A at **600 µg per day from birth to 12 months**, and says chronic intake
above the UL can cause congenital birth defects. A 100 g portion is therefore
about **6.6 times the daily ceiling** — that ratio is our arithmetic on two
issued numbers, not a figure anyone published.

Compared with beef liver, chicken liver is roughly half the vitamin A density
and about twice the iron. On both axes that makes it the better-value liver, and
if you are going to serve one liver a week this is the one to choose. It is
still a food where a small amount does the whole job.

Dividing the UL by the density puts 600 µg at roughly **15 g of simmered chicken
liver**. Again: **arithmetic, not guidance.** No body has issued a gram figure
for infant liver. Treat it as a sense of scale.

**Where the guidance actually stands.** No health body names liver as an infant
food, and none prohibits it. That is an absence of guidance rather than a verdict
either way, and we would rather report the gap than manufacture a bridge across
it. The one issued number that applies to a baby is the 600 µg/day UL; the
once-a-week limit on this page is convention, and the frequency note says whose.

## Prep 6-8

Trim the connective tissue where the two lobes join, and cut away any
green-stained patch — that is bile, and it is bitter enough to ruin the dish.
Do this before cooking.

Simmer the livers gently in water or unsalted stock until they are firm and
uniformly brown through the middle with no red centre. **Cook to 165 °F (USDA
FSIS)** — poultry giblets are explicitly covered by the all-poultry row on the
safe temperature chart, so unlike beef liver this food does have an issued
temperature, and you should use it. Pan-frying works too; simmering just makes
the result easier to blend.

Then puree or mince very fine and stir a **small** amount into a food your baby
already eats — a mashed vegetable, [[oatmeal]], or a mince they are used to.
Chicken liver is milder than beef liver but still strong on its own, and blended
in it is barely detectable.

## Prep 9-11

Same form, same small amount. The pincer grasp does not change the vitamin A
arithmetic, so liver does not graduate to a finger food or to a portion of its
own — it stays an ingredient stirred through something else.

## Prep 12-17

Still blended in, still bounded by the frequency note. One caution as your baby
gets older: the 600 µg/day UL we cite is the figure for **birth to 12 months**.
We have not verified a figure for the year after, so this record does not print
one, and the honest move is to keep the same caution rather than assume the
ceiling rises.

## Safety

**Vitamin A is the hazard, and it is cumulative.** Preformed vitamin A is stored
rather than excreted, so the risk comes from repetition, not from one meal. That
is why this record carries a frequency limit — the only kind of limit in this
canon that says "yes, but not this often."

Read that limit for exactly what it says. The 600 µg/day UL is issued by NIH ODS
and applies to your baby. The once-a-week ceiling is issued by nobody for a baby:
it comes from an NHS page written for the general adult population that does not
mention children, and stretching an adult rule onto a body a fraction of the
weight is an extrapolation. We made it in the cautious direction and labelled it,
because publishing no limit beside a food this dense would have been worse. For a
number fitted to your own baby, ask your pediatrician — it depends on what else
they eat that week.

**Pâté and parfait are excluded**, and for two reasons rather than one. Both are
salt-heavy, and CDC advises against high-sodium foods for children under two;
commercial parfaits and some pâtés are also mould-ripened or set with cultures.
No sodium figure is published here for either, because none was verified against
a primary source while this record was written. Making liver pâté at home does
not solve the salt: the salt is structural to the recipe.

**Cook it through.** 165 °F, measured. Poultry liver is not a food to serve pink,
and undercooked poultry offal is a food-safety problem rather than a texture
preference.

**Texture.** Cooked liver goes grainy and can pack at the back of the mouth.
Pureed or minced very fine, carried in something moist, is both the safe form and
the palatable one.

**Supplements.** A vitamin A-containing supplement or multivitamin counts toward
the same 600 µg ceiling. Check the label before you add liver to the week.
