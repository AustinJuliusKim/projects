## Background

Sardines carry **2.92 mg of iron per 100 g, and it is heme iron** — the readily
absorbed kind. That is the highest iron figure of any food in this batch and
close to three times [[salmon]]'s 1.03. They also carry **382 mg of calcium per
100 g**, which is unusual for a fish and comes almost entirely from the bones.

They add nothing to your allergen coverage that salmon does not — fish is one
slot, and either fish fills it. Sardines are here for the iron and the calcium.

**Now the conflict, because this food has a real one and you should decide about
it with your eyes open.**

CDC's instruction is to "remove all fat, skin and **bones** from poultry, meat
and fish **before cooking**." Canned sardines are sold **bones-in by design**:
the canning process pressure-cooks them until the skeleton is soft and crushable,
and **that softened skeleton is where the 382 mg of calcium comes from.** Remove
the bones and you have removed most of the reason to serve sardines rather than
any other small fish.

**No US body issues an exception for softened canned bones.** There is no
carve-out, no "except when pressure-cooked" clause, nothing. So serving sardines
bones-in is a **departure from a guideline**, done widely and with a good
rationale, and this record labels it as common practice rather than dressing it
up as sanctioned. The cautious version — remove the spine, mash the rest
thoroughly so nothing intact remains, check with your fingers — is what is
described below. The fully guideline-compliant version is to bone the fish and
lose the calcium. Both are defensible. Pick one deliberately.

**On mercury**, FDA and EPA place sardines in **Best Choices**, among the safest
fish available. But their advice is written for **ages 1 to 11** and its smallest
serving is one ounce for a one-to-three-year-old. **No US body publishes a
seafood amount below twelve months**, so this record gives you no infant weekly
figure; any number in circulation was extrapolated by somebody.

**On sodium, read the can.** The USDA entry behind this record shows **307 mg of
sodium per 100 g — and that is the salted pack.** A no-salt-added pack was not
verifiable, so do not read 307 mg as the figure for the tin you bought if it says
no salt added. Sodium is the **only** reason to specify pack type here: nutrition
is otherwise the same. Buy no-salt-added if you can find it, and rinse either
way.

## Prep 6-8

Open the can, drain it, and lift the fish out onto a plate. **Find the spine
first** — it runs the length of the fish, and it is the one bone that can stay
firm enough to matter. Lift it out with a fork or your fingers; it usually comes
away in one piece.

Then mash what is left, thoroughly, with a fork against the plate until it is a
uniform paste and **no intact segment of bone remains** — not softened, not
mostly broken, gone. Run your fingers through it afterwards. Anything you can
feel as a discrete piece comes out.

Stir the mash into something moist: [[oatmeal]], mashed [[sweet-potato]],
[[avocado]], a thick puree. Sardine is strong-tasting, so start with a small
amount stirred through a lot of something familiar rather than a spoonful on its
own.

Rinse the fish first if it is a salted pack.

## Prep 9-11

Coarser mash, still spine-out, still checked by hand. Sardine mixed into a grain
or a vegetable mash is easier to gather than flakes loose on a tray, and it keeps
the taste in proportion.

This is a good age to pair it deliberately: heme iron does not need a vitamin-C
partner the way non-heme does, but the meal it is in usually could use one
anyway — [[broccoli]], [[mango]], [[green-peas]].

## Prep 12-17

However the household eats them — mashed on [[wheat-toast]], through pasta, in a
rice bowl. Still mashed rather than served as a whole fish: a whole sardine, even
canned, is a bone-in fish, and CDC's bones entry does not stop applying because
the child had a birthday.

## Safety

**Bones are the hazard, and this is the one food in this canon where the rule
and the practice genuinely disagree.** "Bones in meat or fish" is a named entry
on CDC's choking-hazard list and CDC says to remove all bones before cooking.
The entire canned-sardine category depends on leaving them in. That is a real
conflict, not an oversight, and no body has resolved it.

What this record recommends: **remove the spine, mash everything else until
nothing is intact, check by hand.** What this record will not tell you: that
softened canned bones are approved for infants, because nobody has said so.

**Rinse a salted pack, and prefer no-salt-added.** 307 mg of sodium per 100 g is
the salted figure. CDC advises against added salt for babies, and this record
cannot give you an infant mg/day limit because **no verified figure was
available** — treat that silence as a reason for caution, not permission.

**Fish is one allergen slot.** If [[salmon]] has already gone in without trouble,
sardines are not a second introduction — they are a nutritional upgrade to a slot
you have already covered.

**No raw, no smoked, no cured.** Canned sardines are cooked in the can, which is
what makes them fine. Salt-cured and smoked small fish are neither cooked nor
low in sodium.
