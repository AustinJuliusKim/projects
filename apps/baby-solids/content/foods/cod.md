## Background

**Cod is not on this list for iron.** At 0.49 mg per 100 g it is the lowest-iron
fish in this set — a third of [[salmon]]'s and a seventh of a serving of red
meat. Its zinc, 0.58 mg, is similarly modest. If you are here to solve iron, cod
is not the tool; [[beef]] or liver is.

What cod is genuinely good for is two things.

**Allergen introduction.** Fish is one of the nine major US allergens, and cod
is the one US labelling law itself reaches for as an example — the statute
illustrates the fish category as "bass, flounder, cod". That makes cod about as
canonical a fish for this purpose as exists. AAP's position is that there is no
evidence delaying fish past four to six months prevents allergy, and FDA states
directly that you can introduce fish at about six months of age. Fish is
introduced like any other food, with the medical gate above for the babies who
need it.

**Texture.** Cooked cod breaks into large, soft, clean flakes that dissolve
under gum pressure. Among first fish that is close to ideal, and it is why cod
is often the easiest fish for a baby who has refused others.

On mercury: FDA and EPA place cod in **Best Choices**, their safest tier. Worth
knowing precisely, though — cod is a Best Choice but is **not** on FDA's shorter
sub-list of Best Choices that are *even lower* in mercury. [[pollock]] and
[[rainbow-trout]] are on that sub-list; cod is not. It is a real distinction and
we would rather show it than flatten it.

## Prep 6-8

Buy it boneless if you can, then go over the raw fillet with your fingers
anyway. Skin and bones come off **before** cooking — that is CDC's order, NHS
says to remove bones from fish, and the Canadian Paediatric Society says plainly
not to feed a baby fish with bones. A raw fillet is also simply easier to search
than a hot cooked one.

Bake, poach, or steam until the flesh is opaque all the way through and separates
into flakes under light pressure. USDA FSIS gives 145 °F for fish and shellfish;
cod hits that about when it stops being translucent.

Flake it fine, run the flakes through your fingers once more, and moisten with a
spoonful of the cooking liquid or a puree so they hold together. Dry cod flakes
powder on the tray and are hard for a six-month-old to gather.

## Prep 9-11

Larger flakes, self-fed. Cod's flake structure suits a pincer grasp well — the
pieces come apart into roughly finger-sized soft chunks that collapse when
squeezed. Still checked by hand for bones every time.

## Prep 12-17

However the family eats it, still deboned and still cooked through. Battered and
deep-fried cod is a different question — the batter carries the salt, not the
fish.

## Safety

**Bones are the whole hazard.** Deboned cod flesh is soft and low-risk. Cod
carries fine bones through the fillet, and both CPS and NHS put fish bones on
the do-not-feed list. Debone before cooking, then check the cooked flakes by
hand. Whole small fish and bone-in steaks are not a form to serve here.

**No salt cod.** 자반대구, bacalao, and any dried or salt-preserved cod are
excluded: they are salt-preservation products, and NHS and CDC both advise
against salt for babies. We publish no sodium figure for salt cod because none
was verified against a primary source — the figure on this page, 78 mg per 100 g,
is for plain cooked cod only.

**No raw fish.** FDA is explicit that children should not eat raw fish,
including the kind found in sushi and sashimi. That covers 대구회 and any raw
preparation.

**How much per week — there is no infant answer.** FDA and EPA's fish advice is
titled for children **ages 1 to 11**. Its smallest serving figure is one ounce at
age one. **No US body publishes a per-week amount for a six-to-eleven-month-old**,
so this record does not invent one. Serve cod as one of several protein foods
across the week, and ask your pediatrician if you want a number for your own
baby.

**One wording point, because two rules look identical and are not.** FDA's fish
advice suggests watching your child for **several days before feeding fish a
second time** — while the same paragraph explicitly rejects delaying allergen
introduction. That sentence is about the gap between the **first and second
exposure to fish itself**, so a delayed reaction to *this* food has time to show.
It is **not** the familiar "wait 3–5 days between new foods" rule, which is a
different claim about *unrelated* foods and is not evidence-based: IgE reactions
happen within minutes to two hours, so spacing out unrelated foods buys nothing
diagnostically and just slows down variety. Keep introducing other new foods on
your normal schedule. We are flagging the tension between FDA's two sentences
rather than resolving it, because FDA has not.
