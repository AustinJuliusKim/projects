## Background

Toran, satoimo, dasheen — a starchy corm that cooks to a smooth, slightly sticky
mash and is a familiar autumn food in Korean and Japanese kitchens. Cooked, it
carries 0.72 mg of iron per 100 g, which is a small contribution; it is here as
a carbohydrate and a cultural food rather than a nutritional one.

**Taro has a specific hazard that is not choking and is not an allergy, and the
usual explanation of it is subtly wrong.** Taro tissue contains calcium oxalate
**raphides** — microscopic needle-shaped crystals. When the plant cells are
damaged by chewing, the needles are propelled out and stab the tongue, gums and
throat. The result is immediate burning, stinging and swelling in the mouth.

The part people get wrong: **cooking destroys the enzymes** that make the sting
worse — which is why cooked taro loses the acrid, prickly taste of raw taro —
**but the raphides themselves are insoluble in water and heat resistant.**
Washing and cooking do not remove the needles. Hong Kong's Centre for Food
Safety states both halves of that plainly.

So thorough cooking is necessary and is what makes edible taro comfortable to
eat. It is not a process that removes the crystals, and that is exactly why the
rules below have no exceptions in them.

This record publishes **no oxalate figure for retail taro.** The number usually
quoted, about 780 mg per 100 g, is FAO's figure for some species of cocoyam, not
a measurement of the taro on a supermarket shelf, and repeating it here would be
inventing a value.

## Prep 6-8

**Peel it completely**, ideally under running water or with gloves — raw taro sap
irritates skin, which is the same mechanism you are avoiding in the mouth.

Boil or steam until a piece collapses under light pressure with no firmness left
anywhere, then mash it and loosen with breast milk, formula, or the cooking
liquid. Taro mash is naturally smooth and a little glutinous, which suits this
age.

**Cook without salt.** USDA's cooked-without-salt entry is 15 mg of sodium per
100 g; the salted entry is **251 mg — about seventeen times more.** That
difference is entirely a cooking decision.

## Prep 9-11

Still peeled, still thoroughly cooked, still mashed — this is one of the few
foods where the texture instruction does not relax with age, because the reason
for it is not grasp or chewing. Mash it into other foods if you want variety:
[[potato]], [[lentils]], a fortified cereal.

## Prep 12-17

Taro as the household cooks it, as long as it is cooked right through and soft.
Torantang and other taro soups are normal family food; take the baby's portion
out before salt or seasoning goes in, and check that every piece is soft — a
piece that is still firm in the middle is undercooked, and undercooked is the
problem.

## Safety

**Never wild taro, under any preparation.** Wild taro and ornamental aroids look
similar to the edible corm and to its leaves. Hong Kong's food safety authority
is explicit that "cooking cannot remove the poisoning effect of wild taro and it
should not be consumed." Poisoning cases there mainly involve wild taro, or
produce suspected of being mixed or wrapped with wild taro leaves. Buy taro as
food, from a food shop.

**Peeled, thoroughly cooked, mashed. That is the whole preparation, and there is
no shortcut inside it.** Raw or undercooked taro is not a texture problem, it is
a raphide exposure.

**If your baby complains of the mouth or throat after taro, treat it as a
raphide exposure, not as fussiness.** Burning, refusal, drooling, pawing at the
mouth or a hoarse cry shortly after eating are what oxalate raphide irritation
looks like, and the reaction is local rather than allergic. Stop the food, offer
something cold to drink, and get medical advice if there is any swelling,
breathing difficulty, or distress that does not settle — mouth and upper-airway
symptoms in a baby are worth a call, not a wait.
