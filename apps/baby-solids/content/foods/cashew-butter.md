## Background

Cashew is a **distinct tree-nut sensitization from almond**, which is why it has
its own record rather than sharing one with [[almond-butter]]. A baby who eats
almond butter without trouble has told you nothing about cashew. The clustering
that is reported runs **cashew with pistachio**, and hazelnut with walnut —
almond sits outside both groups.

That distinctness cuts the other way too, and it is the reason a cashew reaction
is worth taking seriously on its own terms rather than assuming the tree-nut
question was settled by an earlier nut.

**No US body issues a multi-tree-nut introduction protocol.** The number of nuts
that "covers" the tree-nut allergen, the order, and the interval between them
are all unissued. The specialist guidance that exists is explicitly provisional
and waits on trial results. This record covers how to serve cashew butter
safely; it does not tell you how many nuts make a programme, because nobody has
said.

**This record publishes no iron figure and no USDA id.** There is a verified
entry for *dry-roasted cashews* — 6.00 mg of iron and 5.60 mg of zinc per 100 g
— and it appears in the sources as a labelled proxy. It is not printed as cashew
butter's iron. Grinding a nut into butter changes moisture and therefore
per-100 g density, so a whole-nut number carried on a butter record would be
invented. **Cashew butter's own composition is unverified here.**

One question this pre-empts, since the jars invite it: **cashews are never truly
raw.** Retail "raw" cashews have been steamed, because the shell oil is caustic
and has to be driven off. That is not a safety problem for your baby — it is
simply what the word means on that label.

## Prep 6-8

Use **smooth** cashew butter, never crunchy, with as short an ingredient list as
you can find — ideally just cashews.

Put about a quarter teaspoon in a bowl and stir in warm water, breast milk,
formula, or a familiar puree, a little at a time, until **it no longer holds its
shape and pours off the spoon.**

**There is no issued dilution ratio.** The specific one in circulation for nut
butters is issued by nobody. AAP's rule is qualitative — spread thinly. CDC names
the hazard and offers no fix. The one numeric preparation anywhere in US guidance
belongs to NIAID's peanut-allergy-prevention protocol: **written for peanut**, a
dosing instruction rather than a choking rule, and it does not transfer to
cashew. A borrowed number would be the wrong ratio applied to the wrong food.
Thin until it pours.

Offer it in the morning, at home, on an unhurried day, and watch for about two
hours — IgE reactions happen within minutes to two hours.

## Prep 9-11

Same thinned form, or spread very thinly on something soft. **Thickness, not
quantity, is the hazard**: butter stiff enough to keep its shape can obstruct an
airway, so a stiff quarter-teaspoon is worse than a thin tablespoon.

## Prep 12-17

Thinned into [[oatmeal]] or [[yogurt]], stirred through a curry or a dal, spread
very thinly on [[wheat-toast]]. Never off a spoon, never a thick layer.

Cashew appears in a lot of South Asian cooking as **whole or halved nuts** — in
pilaf, in [[semolina-upma]]'s tempering, on top of sweets. Those are pieces of
nut, they are on the hazard list, and they are a different question from the
butter. Grind them in or leave them out.

## Safety

**On the hazard list by name.** AAP: "thick chunks of peanut butter or **other
nut butters**." CDC: "chunks or spoonful of nut and seed butters." Cashew butter
is inside both entries directly.

**Never crunchy**, because a crunchy butter contains nut pieces, which are
separately a hazard.

**Never off a spoon.** NIAID's instruction against nut butter from a spoon or in
lumps under four years is written for peanut; applying it here is this record's
judgement and is tiered as expert opinion. The mechanism is identical.

**Whole cashews are not appropriate at any age in this app's range.** NIAID
puts whole nuts at **under 5 years**; AAP's framing is "until 4 years of age or
older." **The two disagree, both are live, and NIAID's five-year floor is the
cautious default.** This is stated rather than quietly resolved. The butter,
thinned, is fine from six months — the age floor is about the nut.

**Watch for cashew hidden in adult food.** Cashew is a common thickener in
restaurant curries and sauces, where it is blended in and invisible. That is
worth knowing after a reaction, not before one: if something happened and you
are trying to work out what was in the dish, cashew paste is a frequent unlisted
answer.
