## Background

**No guideline body names Korean or Asian pear.** AAP, CDC, NHS and Health
Canada all cover it only at the category level — hard raw fruit — so everything
below is that category rule applied to this fruit, not a ruling anyone issued
about *Pyrus pyrifolia*. That distinction is worth stating plainly, because the
category rule it lands in is a strict one.

**Do not carry over the logic from [[pear]].** A European pear is safe raw once
it ripens because ripening softens it. A Korean pear does not do that. It stays
crisp and dense at full ripeness — that crunch is the point of the fruit — which
puts it squarely inside AAP's "raw fruit chunks, such as apple pieces" entry
rather than inside NHS's permission for soft ripe fruit. So it inherits the
[[apple]] rules: cook until fork-mashable, or grate raw, peel either way.

**Iron is zero.** Not low — zero, verified in USDA's entry, alongside 3.8 mg of
vitamin C per 100 g. This is a hydration and flavour food. It is very juicy,
mildly sweet, and pleasant on sore gums when grated; it contributes nothing to
the 11 mg of iron a day a 7–12 month old needs, and it is not strong enough on
vitamin C to serve as the partner for an iron meal. Use it for enjoyment and
variety, and get iron from [[tofu]], [[barley]] cereal, or [[green-peas]].

**Two Korean-kitchen forms that are not appropriate for a baby.** Grated bae is
the standard tenderiser in bulgogi and galbi marinades, mixed with soy sauce and
sugar — that combination is high sodium and added sugar, both off the menu under
24 months. And bae juice, or baesuk simmered with honey, runs into three
separate rules: no juice before 12 months, no added sugars before 24, no honey
before 12. Serve the fruit plain.

## Prep 6-8

**Grated.** This is the easier route and the one that suits the fruit. Peel it,
then grate on the coarse side of a box grater. The shreds are short, wet, and
soft enough to gum. Serve on a loaded spoon or stirred into yogurt or porridge.

**Cooked.** Peel, core, slice, and simmer with a splash of water until a slice
collapses under a fork. It takes noticeably longer than an apple — a dense
Korean pear can want fifteen to twenty minutes — so test rather than time it.
Serve as finger-length slices held in the fist with an end sticking out.

## Prep 9-11

The pincer grasp, most often from 9 to 10 months per AAP, means cooked cubes
about the size of your thumbnail can be picked up one at a time. They still have
to be cooked soft. Being able to lift a raw cube is not the same as being able
to chew one.

Grated raw stays the low-effort option, and now that the pincer grip exists a
small pile of shreds is something the baby can manage alone.

## Prep 12-17

Cooked soft or grated raw, still peeled. Raw chunks and raw batons are the part
that does not change at twelve months — AAP's line for raw fruit chunks is four
years.

If the household is eating bae at the end of a meal, grate the toddler's portion
off the same fruit rather than handing over a slice.

## Safety

The specific trap here is that it looks like a pear and is sold as a pear, so it
invites the pear reasoning: wait until it is ripe, then serve it raw. That
reasoning is wrong for this fruit. Ripeness does not soften it, and a ripe
Korean pear cut into cubes is the same hazard as raw apple cubes — hard, smooth,
and breaking into airway-sized pieces under a bite.

Peel it at every age in this range. The skin is tougher than a European pear's
and does not break down under gums.

Serve it plain: no marinade, no juice, no honey.
