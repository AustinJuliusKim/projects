## Background

Dark turkey meat is the quiet upgrade on [[chicken-thigh]]. Roasted, it carries
1.43 mg of iron and 3.51 mg of zinc per 100 g against chicken thigh's 1.13 mg
iron and 1.92 mg zinc — about 27% more iron and roughly 83% more zinc, for a
food that cooks the same way and tastes almost the same to a baby. The zinc gap
is the more interesting one.

**These figures are for dark meat only.** Thigh, leg, and drumstick meat — not
breast. Turkey breast is a different food nutritionally, and carrying these
numbers across to it would overstate what a breast portion delivers. If you buy
a whole bird, the dark meat is the part this record is about.

The iron is heme iron, the readily absorbed form. As with [[beef]] and lamb, it
does not need a vitamin-C food beside it.

One honesty note on form. AAP names "well-cooked, finely chopped chicken" as a
finger food — unusually specific for a guideline body, which mostly describes
textures rather than dishes. No body says the same sentence about turkey.
Extending chicken's phrasing to turkey is our inference and common practice, not
an issued instruction; it is a reasonable one, because the two behave
identically in a pan, but you should know which is which.

## Prep 6-8

Skin, fat, and bones come off **before** cooking — CDC's order, and it keeps
bone fragments out of the finished meat entirely. Braise, poach, or roast the
thigh or leg until it pulls apart with a fork.

Cook all poultry to 165 °F (USDA FSIS), which explicitly covers legs and thighs.
Then chop the meat finely and stir in enough unsalted broth or pan juice that it
holds together on a spoon instead of scattering.

Run the chopped meat between your fingers before it goes on the tray. Turkey leg
in particular carries hard tendons that a fork will not catch and a baby cannot
chew.

## Prep 9-11

Shredded rather than chopped. Long soft strands suit a pincer grasp better than
crumbs, and dark meat shreds cleanly once it is cooked through. Stirred into
[[oatmeal]] or mashed vegetables it stays moist longer than it does alone.

## Prep 12-17

However the family eats it, still soft, still cut small, still deboned. Sliced
deli turkey is a different product and does not belong here — it is cured or
brined and carries the sodium that comes with that.

## Safety

**Buy an unenhanced bird.** This is the purchasing instruction that actually
changes the numbers. Turkey sold "with added solution", "enhanced", "self-
basting", or brine-injected is water, salt and phosphate injected into the meat,
and USDA's own records for those products show roughly 25% *less* iron per 100 g
than plain roasted dark meat, plus the added sodium — while CDC advises against
high-sodium foods under two years. You lose on both counts at once. Read the
label on the package: the added-solution declaration is required to be there,
usually in small print near the weight.

**Tendons and bones.** Turkey legs carry more and tougher tendons than chicken.
Debone before cooking, then check the chopped or shredded meat by hand. Bones in
meat are on CDC's hazard list and do not become safe by being small.

**Chunks.** "Tough or large chunks of meat" stays on the hazard list past the
first birthday. Finely chopped or shredded is the form, at every age in this
range.

**Doneness.** 165 °F, measured, not judged by colour. Turkey thigh meat stays
slightly rosy near the bone even when fully cooked, so colour will mislead you
in both directions here.
