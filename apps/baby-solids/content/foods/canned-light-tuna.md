## Background

Canned light tuna is the most convenient iron in this set — 1.53 mg per 100 g in
the no-salt-added version, no cooking, no bones — and it is also the food on this
list where the wrong tin is genuinely a different safety decision. Read the
species notes below before you buy.

**"Light" is a mercury tier, not a species.** FDA says canned light tuna can be a
mix of generally smaller tuna species, most often skipjack. That is why FDA
places **canned light tuna in Best Choices** — its safest tier. Note the
precision, though: canned light tuna is a Best Choice but is **not** on FDA's
shorter sub-list of Best Choices that are *even lower* in mercury.
[[rainbow-trout]] and [[pollock]] are on that sub-list; this is not.

**The other tunas are not interchangeable with it.**

| What the tin or menu says | FDA tier | What that means |
|---|---|---|
| Light / skipjack | **Best Choice** | The subject of this record |
| Albacore, or "white" tuna | **Good Choice** | Typically three times more mercury than canned light |
| Yellowfin | **Good Choice** | |
| Bigeye | **Choice to Avoid** | FDA's highest-mercury tier |

For a Good Choice, FDA's instruction is one serving that week **and no other
fish at all that week** — a much tighter rule than it looks, because it removes
every other fish from the week, not just other tuna. That advice is written for
children ages 1 to 11.

**"Ahi" tells you nothing.** FDA's Seafood List records "Ahi" as a *prohibited*
vernacular name for **both** bigeye tuna (a Choice to Avoid) and yellowfin tuna
(a Good Choice), and both species lawfully carry the single market name "Tuna".
So "ahi" on a menu or at a counter carries no tier information at all — it could
be either. This is a naming fact, not an inference from one.

## Prep 6-8

Buy **no-salt-added** light tuna canned in water. Drain it, then flake it fine
and stir it into a food your baby already eats — a mashed vegetable, plain
[[yogurt]], [[oatmeal]], or a puree — until it is properly wet rather than merely
damp.

There is no cooking temperature on this page and none is missing: canned tuna is
retort-sterilised in the tin, so it arrives cooked. Nothing needs to reach a
target temperature. If you warm it, you are doing that for palatability.

Unlike the other four fish here, there is no bone step. Canned light tuna is
already flaked and boneless.

## Prep 9-11

Same form. A pincer grasp still cannot pick up dry flakes usefully, so the
moistening does not stop being necessary — bound into a puree or mashed
[[avocado]] it becomes something a baby can actually get to their mouth.

## Prep 12-17

Mixed into a soft salad, a rice bowl, or a spread, still no-salt-added and still
moist. Regular canned tuna, tuna in brine, and seasoned 참치캔 do not become
appropriate at twelve months; the sodium guidance runs to twenty-four.

## Safety

**Buy no-salt-added, and understand that draining does not fix it.** This is the
most actionable purchasing instruction in this set of records. USDA's figures:
regular light tuna canned in water and drained carries **219 mg of sodium per
100 g**; the no-salt-added version carries **50 mg**. That is a **4.4× gap, and
it is measured after draining** — the salt is in the fish, not floating in the
water you pour off. CDC advises against high-sodium foods for children under two,
and NHS says babies should not eat salty foods. One label choice at the shop
closes the gap entirely; nothing you do in the kitchen does.

**Never substitute albacore.** Albacore is sold as "white" tuna, sits beside
light tuna on the shelf at a similar price, and looks like an upgrade. FDA states
that it typically contains **three times more mercury** than canned light tuna,
and tiers it as a Good Choice rather than a Best Choice, with the "no other fish
that week" restriction attached. It is not a swap for this food. Neither is
yellowfin, and bigeye is on FDA's Avoid list outright.

**Texture is the choking consideration, not bones.** Canned tuna is boneless, so
the bone rule that governs the other four fish does not apply. Its risk is
different: drained tuna is dry and powdery and can pack at the back of the mouth.
Moistening it is the modification, and that instruction is common practice rather
than a rule any guideline body issued — but it is the difference between a food a
baby eats and one they gag on.

**Mercury does not cook out.** FDA states that mercury is found throughout the
tissue of a fish, so cleaning or cooking will not reduce it. The species decision
is made at the shop and cannot be undone at the stove.

**How much per week — there is no infant answer.** FDA and EPA's advice is titled
for children **ages 1 to 11**, and its smallest figure is one ounce at age one.
**No US body publishes a per-week amount for a six-to-eleven-month-old**, so this
record does not invent one. Ask your pediatrician if you want a number for your
own baby.

One divergence worth knowing above twelve months: **NHS states that fresh and
canned tuna do not count as oily fish**, so its sex-based oily-fish caps for
children — boys up to 4 portions a week, girls no more than 2, set on dioxins and
PCBs — do not apply to canned tuna. FDA does not address that classification at
all. Neither framework reaches below twelve months.

**One wording point, because two rules look identical and are not.** FDA's fish
advice suggests watching your child for **several days before feeding fish a
second time**, while the same paragraph explicitly rejects delaying allergen
introduction. That sentence is about the gap between the **first and second
exposure to fish itself**. It is **not** the familiar "wait 3–5 days between new
foods" rule, which is about *unrelated* foods and is not evidence-based: IgE
reactions happen within minutes to two hours. Keep introducing other new foods on
your normal schedule. The tension between FDA's two sentences is real and we are
flagging it rather than resolving it.
