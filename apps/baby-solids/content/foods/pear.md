## Background

Pear is the food on this list where the label on the shelf tells you nothing and
your thumb tells you everything. **Ripeness decides the whole record.**

A ripe European pear — one that gives when you press near the stem — is soft
enough that NHS's permission for soft raw fruit applies directly. Low risk,
peeled, no cooking.

An underripe pear is a hard raw fruit, and everything in [[apple]] applies to it
unchanged: cook until fork-mashable, or grate it raw, peel either way. Pears are
usually sold hard because they travel and store better that way, so most pears
you bring home are in this second category for a few days.

Nutritionally it is a trace contributor — 0.18 mg of iron and 4.3 mg of vitamin
C per 100 g. Serve it because it is soft, sweet, and easy, not because it moves
any number.

One thing this record deliberately does not claim: pear is often described as a
remedy or a trigger for infant constipation because of its sorbitol content. **No
primary-source guideline addresses pear, sorbitol, and infant stool**, so there
is no claim here in either direction. If stooling is a worry, that is a question
for your pediatrician rather than a fruit choice.

See also [[korean-pear]], which looks like a pear and behaves like an apple.

## Prep 6-8

Check ripeness first. Press the flesh just below the stem with your thumb. If it
yields, it is ripe.

**Ripe:** peel it, quarter it, remove the core, and cut finger-length slices.
The whole-hand grasp means the baby holds the slice in a fist and bites the
protruding end. Ripe pear is slippery once peeled — leaving a small band of skin
at the gripping end gives some purchase, but the eating end should be peeled.

**Firm:** peel, core, and simmer or roast until a slice collapses under a fork,
then serve the same way. Grating raw works too and needs no pan.

## Prep 9-11

With the pincer grasp — most often from 9 to 10 months per AAP — soft ripe pear
cut into thumbnail-sized cubes is easy to pick up, and peeled. Firm pear still
gets cooked or grated first; the pincer grip changes what the baby can lift, not
what they can chew.

## Prep 12-17

However the rest of the table is eating it, provided it is ripe and peeled. Firm
pear still wants cooking or grating.

## Safety

The mistake to avoid is treating "pear" as a single food. A rock-hard pear cut
into cubes is the same hazard shape as raw apple, and the fact that it is a pear
does not change that.

Peel it at every age in this range. Pear skin is thin but does not break down
under gums, and NHS notes that removing skin makes food easier to swallow.

Whole pear halves and long unpeeled wedges thick enough to snap into a chunk are
not appropriate; slices and cubes are.
