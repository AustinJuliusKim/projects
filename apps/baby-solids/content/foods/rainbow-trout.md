## Background

Rainbow trout sits in FDA and EPA's **Best Choices** tier *and* on their shorter
sub-list of Best Choices that are **even lower in mercury** — the safest
classification FDA issues, shared here with [[pollock]] and [[salmon]] but not
with [[cod]] or canned light tuna.

Its iron is modest: 0.36 mg per 100 g farmed, 0.38 mg wild. That 0.02 mg
difference between farmed and wild is not worth choosing on — buy whichever is
fresher. Zinc is 0.54 mg. Like [[cod]], trout earns its place on allergen
introduction, on mercury standing, and on flavour rather than on minerals; if
iron is the goal, [[beef]] or liver does far more.

Fish is one of the nine major US allergens. AAP's position is that there is no
evidence delaying fish past four to six months prevents allergy, and FDA states
directly that fish can be introduced at about six months of age.

## Prep 6-8

**Trout takes the most deboning work of any fish in this set**, mostly because of
how it is sold: usually whole or butterflied, with the frame and the fine bones
still in place, rather than as a clean fillet. Budget real time for this.

Take the skin off and lift out the bones from the raw flesh, running your
fingers along the whole fillet rather than only the spine line. This happens
**before** cooking — CDC's order, NHS says to remove bones from fish, and the
Canadian Paediatric Society says plainly not to feed a baby fish with bones. The
practical reason is that raw flesh holds together while you search it and hot
cooked flesh falls apart around the bone you are trying to find.

Bake, poach, or pan-cook until the flesh is opaque through and flakes cleanly.
USDA FSIS gives 145 °F for fish and shellfish.

Flake fine, search the flakes by hand once more, and moisten with cooking liquid
or a puree so they hold together instead of powdering.

## Prep 9-11

Larger flakes, self-fed, still hand-searched. Trout is mild enough to serve
fairly plain at this age, and stirred through rice or a mashed vegetable it is
easier to gather than loose flakes on a tray.

## Prep 12-17

However the family eats it, still deboned and still cooked through. Whole
pan-fried trout on the bone is the classic presentation and it is the one form
not to hand a toddler.

## Safety

**Bones, and more of them than usual.** Deboned trout flesh is soft and
low-risk; the bones are the whole hazard, and trout's presentation puts more of
them in front of you than any other fish here. Debone before cooking, search the
cooked flakes by hand. The tweezers technique is common practice — the issued
rule is only that bones come out, and that they come out before cooking.

**A caught-fish rule that is easy to over-apply.** FDA's advice singles trout out
in its guidance on **fish you or someone you know caught**: larger carp,
catfish, trout and perch are more likely to have local fish advisories, and where
no advisory exists, "children under the age of six should limit their consumption
of these fish to 1 to 2 ounces per week."

That is the only ounce figure in the whole advice whose wording reaches below age
one, so it gets quoted a lot — usually wrongly. **It is written for caught fish,
not for farmed retail trout**, and this record does not generalise it. If you
bought your trout at a shop, it is not the fish that sentence is about. If
someone caught it, check your state or provincial advisories first, because those
are issued per water body and FDA's national list does not replace them.

**No raw trout.** FDA is explicit that children should not eat raw fish,
including sushi and sashimi. Cold-smoked trout is not cooked either, and smoked
trout is salt-cured besides.

**Mercury does not cook out.** FDA states that mercury is found throughout the
tissue of a fish, so cleaning or cooking will not reduce it.

**How much per week — no infant answer, and two bodies disagree above twelve
months.** FDA and EPA's advice is titled for children **ages 1 to 11**; its
smallest figure is one ounce at age one, and **no US body publishes a per-week
amount for a six-to-eleven-month-old**. Above the first birthday:

- **FDA/EPA** tier on **methylmercury** alone, with no sex-specific child cap.
- **NHS** caps oily fish for children **by sex** — boys up to 4 portions a week,
  girls no more than 2 — on **dioxins and PCBs**, and names trout as an oily
  fish.

Neither reaches below twelve months. We are surfacing the disagreement rather
than picking a winner; NHS's construction is the more cautious of the two.

**One wording point, because two rules look identical and are not.** FDA's fish
advice suggests watching your child for **several days before feeding fish a
second time**, while the same paragraph explicitly rejects delaying allergen
introduction. That sentence is about the gap between the **first and second
exposure to fish itself**. It is **not** the familiar "wait 3–5 days between new
foods" rule, which is about *unrelated* foods and is not evidence-based: IgE
reactions happen within minutes to two hours. Keep introducing other new foods on
your normal schedule. The tension between FDA's two sentences is real and we are
flagging it rather than resolving it.
