## Background

🚩 **숙주 is not soy, and it is not a major allergen.** Mung bean is a different
plant from soybean, and it appears nowhere on the list of nine major US food
allergens.

That is worth stating clearly and right at the top, because **[[soybean-sprouts]]
(콩나물) *is* soy — one of the nine** — and the two sprouts look nearly identical
in a bowl. This is the pairing most likely to cause a mistake in a Korean
kitchen. The difference is at the head end: 숙주 has a small pale head and a
short plump stem; 콩나물 has an unmistakable yellow soybean on top, bigger and
firmer, on a longer stem. If someone in the family is avoiding soy, that is the
distinction to learn, and it is the same comparison written from the other side
on the 콩나물 page.

**숙주 is also the one food in this batch that a US guideline body names
directly** — twice, in fact. Almost everything else here is a category rule we
have applied by inference. This one is not: FDA names mung bean sprouts
explicitly, both as an example of sprouts that may carry bacteria and again in
the list of sprouts that vulnerable people should avoid raw or lightly cooked.
That makes it the strongest food-specific citation on this whole set of pages.

**Composition figures are not in this record.** The USDA database was
unavailable when it was researched, so no iron or vitamin value is printed here.
The iron in a sprout is **non-heme** — a classification, not a measurement.

## Prep 6-8

🚨 **Cook it all the way through. Raw is out, and so is lightly cooked.**

FDA's reasoning is specific to how sprouts are grown: seeds and beans sprouted
in warm, humid conditions, which are also ideal for bacteria — so a few
organisms present on the seed multiply to high levels during sprouting, **even
if you grow them yourself under clean conditions at home.** The named pathogens
are *Salmonella*, *Listeria* and *E. coli*. FDA's instruction is that people
more vulnerable to foodborne illness should avoid **"raw or lightly cooked
sprouts of any kind (including onion, alfalfa, clover, radish, and mung bean
sprouts)"** — and the vulnerable list opens with **children**.

**"Lightly cooked" being prohibited alongside raw is the part that catches
people.** 숙주나물 is traditionally made by dunking the sprouts in boiling water
for well under a minute to keep them crunchy. That is precisely the preparation
FDA rules out. **A blanch is not the mitigation.** Cook them until they are
genuinely soft — a few minutes of simmering or steaming, not a dip.

Then:

1. **Trim the root tails** — the fine threadlike ends. They stay tough and
   stringy however long you cook them. Pinching them off is standard practice
   for 숙주나물 anyway.
2. **Chop short — no longer than half an inch.** That is AAP's rule and the only
   dimensional rule any US guideline body issues. The hazard with a sprout is
   its *length*: a cooked stem is a long slippery fibrous string.
3. **Chop fine and moisten** at this age, stirred through rice, [[oatmeal]] or a
   puree so it clumps rather than scattering across the tray.

Unseasoned. 숙주나물 is normally dressed with salt, sesame oil and garlic — take
the baby's portion out before any of that. Note that sesame oil brings a major
allergen with it, which matters if that is one you are tracking.

## Prep 9-11

Same cooking, same tails, same length limit. Chopped pieces suit the pincer
grasp that most babies develop around nine to ten months. Better hand skills are
not a reason to serve longer strands — the string is the hazard, and picking it
up was never the difficult part.

## Prep 12-17

숙주 as the family eats it, still **cooked through** and still **cut short**.
FDA's rule is about susceptibility rather than age, so it does not lapse at the
first birthday — raw 숙주 on top of 쌀국수 or in a salad is still not the version
to serve.

Cooked into 잡채, soups or a stir-fry it is easy at this age. Watch the seasoning
rather than the sprout.

## Safety

🚨 **Raw and lightly cooked are both out — and this is FDA naming this exact
food.** Not an inference, not a category rule stretched to fit: mung bean
sprouts appear by name in FDA's sprout guidance, twice, and children head the
list of people who should avoid them raw or lightly cooked. Cook them properly.
Washing does not fix a sprout, because the bacteria grow throughout it rather
than sitting on the surface.

**Strings.** Long fibrous stems are the mechanical hazard. Trim the tails, chop
to under half an inch.

**Not a major allergen** — mung bean is not soy. But **do not confuse it with
콩나물**, which is. If you are tracking a soy introduction or avoiding soy
entirely, check which sprout is actually in the bag.

**Watch what it is dressed with.** The sprout is plain; 숙주나물 usually is not.
Sesame oil is a major allergen and salt is the usual seasoning.

**Beyond FDA's sprout rule, no body addresses 숙주 as an infant food.** The
cooking prohibition and the half-inch limit are issued. The tail trimming and
the chop length applied here are ordinary practice.
