## Background

This record is about one species: ***Scomber scombrus***, which FDA calls
**Atlantic mackerel**. Naming matters more here than for any other food in this
canon, so the species is on the page and stays on the page.

Nutritionally it is the best of the five fish here for iron — 1.57 mg per 100 g,
about three times [[cod]]'s and half again [[salmon]]'s — with 0.94 mg zinc and
83 mg sodium. On mercury, FDA and EPA place Atlantic mackerel in **Best
Choices**, and further, on the shorter sub-list of Best Choices that are *even
lower* in mercury. That is the safest classification FDA issues.

### 고등어 is usually a different mackerel, and you should know which

**Korean 고등어 is normally *Scomber japonicus*, which FDA calls Pacific chub
mackerel — not *Scomber scombrus*.** They are close relatives and they look
similar on ice, but they are not the same fish, and we are not going to pretend
otherwise on a page a parent uses.

What this changes and what it does not:

- **Safety tier: identical.** Pacific chub mackerel is also a **Best Choice**.
  If you buy 고등어 from a Korean grocer, you have bought a Best Choice fish.
- **Sub-list: different.** Only Atlantic mackerel is on FDA's "even lower in
  mercury" sub-list. Pacific chub mackerel is a Best Choice but is not on it.
- **Composition: we have no verified figures.** The numbers on this page come
  from USDA record 175120, which is *S. scombrus*. **No verified USDA record for
  cooked *S. japonicus* was located**, so if the fish in your pan is 고등어, treat
  the iron, zinc and sodium here as the closest available reference rather than a
  measurement of what you are cooking.

Everything else on this page — bones, cooking, salt-cured forms, raw forms —
applies to both fish equally.

### The label trap: three tiers collide on the word "Spanish"

FDA's Seafood List sets acceptable market names for seafood and marks other
names as prohibited vernaculars. Read together, they produce a genuine hazard:

- FDA's own acceptable market name for **King mackerel** — *Scomberomorus
  cavalla*, which is a **Choice to Avoid**, FDA's highest-mercury tier — is
  **"Mackerel, King or Spanish"**. A package lawfully labelled "Spanish
  mackerel" may therefore be King mackerel.
- "Spanish" is *also* a prohibited vernacular for **Pacific chub mackerel**, a
  Best Choice.
- And "Mackerel, Spanish" is the market name for *Scomberomorus maculatus*,
  Atlantic Spanish mackerel, which FDA tiers as a **Good Choice**.

So the words "Spanish mackerel" on a package can point at an Avoid fish, a Good
fish, or a Best fish. **The label is ambiguous and carries no reliable tier
information.** "Common mackerel" is no better — it maps to both Atlantic and
Pacific chub mackerel.

The practical instruction: buy mackerel where you can establish the species, and
do not treat "mackerel" as one food. If the only thing you know is the word on
the package, you do not know which tier you have.

## Prep 6-8

Mackerel is the boniest fish in this set. Fillet it or buy it filleted, then take
the skin off and go over the raw flesh with your fingers along the whole length,
not just along the spine line — the fine bones run through the fillet. All of
this happens **before** cooking: that is CDC's order, NHS says to remove bones
from fish, and the Canadian Paediatric Society says plainly not to feed a baby
fish with bones. Raw flesh is also far easier to search than hot cooked flesh.

Bake, grill, or pan-cook until the flesh is opaque through and flakes apart.
USDA FSIS gives 145 °F for fish and shellfish.

Flake it fine, run the flakes through your fingers one more time, and moisten
with cooking liquid or a puree so they hold together. Mackerel is oilier than
cod, so it needs less added moisture — but it needs more searching.

## Prep 9-11

Larger flakes, self-fed, still hand-searched every time. Mackerel's strong
flavour lands better mixed into rice or [[oatmeal]] than served plain, which
also makes it easier to gather.

## Prep 12-17

However the family eats it, still deboned and still cooked through. Grilled
고등어구이 made without salt is fine; the salted version is not — see below.

## Safety

**Bones, more than any other fish here.** Deboned mackerel flesh is soft and
low-risk; the bones are the entire hazard, and mackerel has a lot of them
distributed through the fillet. Debone before cooking, then search the cooked
flakes by hand. The tweezers-and-fingertips technique is common practice, not a
rule anyone issued — the issued rule is simply that the bones come out, and that
they come out before cooking.

**No salt-cured mackerel.** 자반고등어 is salt-preserved, and salt is advised
against for babies by both NHS and CDC. We publish no sodium figure for it,
because none was verified against a primary source; the 83 mg per 100 g on this
page is plain cooked fish only.

**No raw mackerel.** FDA is explicit that children should not eat raw fish,
including sushi and sashimi. That rules out 고등어회.

**Mercury does not cook out.** FDA states directly that mercury is found
throughout the tissue of a fish, so cleaning or cooking will not reduce it. This
matters here because the species question above cannot be fixed in the kitchen —
if you bought the wrong mackerel, cooking it does not make it a Best Choice.

**How much per week — no infant answer, and two bodies disagree above twelve
months.** FDA and EPA's advice is titled for children **ages 1 to 11**, and its
smallest figure is one ounce at age one; **no US body publishes a per-week amount
for a six-to-eleven-month-old**, so this record does not invent one. Above the
first birthday the two frameworks diverge and it is worth knowing which you are
reading:

- **FDA/EPA** tier fish on **methylmercury** alone and issue no sex-specific cap
  for children — two servings a week from Best Choices.
- **NHS** caps oily fish for children **by sex** — boys up to 4 portions a week,
  girls no more than 2 — on **dioxins and PCBs**, a different contaminant
  entirely, because of pollutants that could accumulate before a future
  pregnancy. NHS names mackerel as an oily fish.

Neither framework reaches below twelve months. We are surfacing the disagreement
rather than picking a winner; the more cautious construction is NHS's, since it
adds a limit FDA does not.

**One wording point, because two rules look identical and are not.** FDA's fish
advice suggests watching your child for **several days before feeding fish a
second time**, while the same paragraph explicitly rejects delaying allergen
introduction. That sentence is about the gap between the **first and second
exposure to fish itself**. It is **not** the familiar "wait 3–5 days between new
foods" rule, which is about *unrelated* foods and is not evidence-based: IgE
reactions happen within minutes to two hours. Keep introducing other new foods
on your normal schedule. The tension between FDA's two sentences is real and we
are flagging it rather than resolving it.
