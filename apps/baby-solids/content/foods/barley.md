## Background

FDA names barley explicitly as an alternative to rice cereal in its
inorganic-arsenic guidance, and CDC recommends offering a variety of fortified
infant cereals — oat, barley, multi-grain — rather than only rice. That is why
barley is here.

**The wheat and gluten question, stated exactly, because it is routinely
muddled.**

Barley is **not wheat** and is **not one of the nine US major allergens**. It
carries no US allergen labelling requirement, and a child with an IgE wheat
allergy is not, by that fact, allergic to barley.

Barley **is** a gluten-containing grain. FDA's gluten-free rule defines those as
"wheat, rye, barley, and other grains produced by breeding" them, so a food
containing barley cannot be labelled gluten-free.

Put together: **barley is a celiac question, not a wheat-allergy question.** A
child with celiac disease must avoid it. But do not read "not wheat" as "safe
for a wheat-allergic child" and serve it on that basis — if there is a diagnosed
wheat allergy in the picture, that decision belongs with the clinician managing
it, not with a labelling technicality.

**On timing, there is nothing to wait for.** ESPGHAN's position is that gluten
may be introduced at any point between 4 and 12 completed months, and that
timing does not affect the cumulative incidence of celiac disease — a position
its 2024 update maintains. No delay is warranted, and delaying does not prevent
anything.

**Fortification is the entire nutritional story, and the gap is about 36-fold.**
Dry fortified barley infant cereal carries **48.2 mg of iron per 100 g**. Cooked
pearled barley carries 1.33 mg. Hulled barley is 3.6 mg raw and pearled 2.5 mg
raw, both before the dilution of cooking. Against an iron requirement of 11 mg a
day at 7 to 12 months, fortified infant cereal is the single realistic vehicle
short of meat — the 48.2 mg figure is for the dry powder, so what actually
reaches the bowl depends on how thickly you mix it, but the ratio holds.

Added iron absorbs inefficiently, so pair it with a vitamin-C food in the same
meal: [[mango]], [[green-peas]], [[butternut-squash]]. Do not pair it with
[[yogurt]] — dairy calcium inhibits non-heme absorption.

**Prepare it with breast milk, formula, or water. Not cow's milk before 12
months.** USDA's "prepared" barley cereal entry is made up with whole milk,
which is not the preparation to copy at this age.

## Prep 6-8

Fortified infant barley cereal: put a spoonful in a bowl and mix with breast
milk, formula, or water until it is smooth and thick enough to stay on a loaded
spoon. Start thinner and thicken it over the following weeks — that transition
is the point of the food as much as the iron is.

Whole barley is a different job. Pearled barley needs simmering for the better
part of an hour, well past the chewy texture an adult would want, and then
grinding or mashing. CDC's instruction for whole grains is to cook and finely
grind or mash the kernels; a whole cooked barley grain is on the hazard list as
written.

Barley cereal takes other foods well — mashed [[butternut-squash]], puréed
[[spinach]], mashed [[tofu]] — which is a good way to get the vitamin-C pairing
and the iron into the same bowl.

## Prep 9-11

Thicker, lumpier cereal, and this is the age Health Canada flags for it: lumpy
textures should be offered no later than nine months. Cooked-and-mashed pearled
barley folded into the cereal is a natural step up from smooth.

Barley porridge with soft vegetables and a mashed protein is a full meal at this
age, and a spoon the baby holds themselves is worth the mess.

## Prep 12-17

However the household eats it — boribap, barley in a soup or stew, barley
porridge. Keep grains soft and well cooked, and mash or chop through anything
that is still distinctly chewy.

Roasted barley tea (boricha) is a common household drink but it is a drink, and
before twelve months drinks other than breast milk or formula displace what a
baby actually needs. It also carries no nutrition worth counting.

## Safety

**Whole cooked grain kernels are on CDC's choking-hazard list.** Barley grains
are small but firm and slippery, and pearled barley cooked to an adult's
"al dente" is exactly the texture the list is about. Cook it soft, then grind or
mash.

**Gluten, not wheat allergy.** Barley belongs in a conversation about celiac
disease. It does not belong in a wheat-free diet, and it should not be presented
as a safe substitute for a wheat-allergic child without a clinician's say-so.

**Never cow's milk to mix it before 12 months.** Breast milk, formula or water
only. This is about volume of unmodified cow's milk, not about dairy in general
— see [[yogurt]], which is fine from around six months.
