## Background

A plain masa tortilla, warmed until it is soft and floppy, is one of the easier
things to hand a baby: it tears rather than snaps, it holds other food, and it
carries 1.40 mg of iron per 100 g.

**Nixtamalization — what it actually does, because the usual version of this
story is wrong.** Cooking corn in lime before grinding it into masa raises the
calcium enormously: whole corn grain measures **7 mg of calcium per 100 g**, a
corn tortilla **175 mg** — roughly **twenty-five times**, and that calcium comes
from the lime itself. That part is a straightforward measurement.

The niacin claim, as it is usually told, does not survive the data. **Total
niacin falls**: corn grain 3.63 mg per 100 g, tortilla 1.5 mg. What
nixtamalization changes is **bioavailability** — maize niacin is bound up as
niacytin and largely unavailable, and alkaline processing releases it into a
form the body can absorb. FAO's own maize monograph says both things: niacin is
"slightly more available from lime-treated maize than from raw maize," and
processing losses of niacin are real.

So the accurate sentence is: **nixtamalization sharply raises calcium and
releases bound niacin into an absorbable form; it does not raise total niacin.**
**How much more absorbable is not quantified in anything verifiable**, so this
record publishes no figure for it.

None of this makes a tortilla an iron food on the scale of
[[fortified-infant-cereal]]. It is a useful, calcium-bearing carbohydrate and a
good vehicle for mashed [[black-beans]] or [[avocado]].

## Prep 6-8

Warm a plain tortilla on a dry pan just until it is pliable and steamy — **not
until it browns, crisps or blisters hard.** Tear it into strips a couple of
fingers wide that the baby can hold with the whole hand, and serve it with
something wet: mashed [[avocado]], puréed [[black-beans]], a spoonful of
[[plantain]].

A dry tortilla left in the mouth can gum together into a wad, which is the
realistic hazard at this age rather than a hard chunk. Keeping it damp and
served alongside something soft solves it.

**Buy the no-added-salt version if you can find it.** USDA's unsalted tortilla is
11 mg of sodium per 100 g against 45 mg for the salted one — small numbers on
their own, but babies do not need added salt and it accumulates across a day.

## Prep 9-11

Same tortilla, smaller pieces once the pincer grasp arrives, and it becomes a
useful wrapper: a soft tortilla folded around mashed beans and soft vegetables
is a whole meal that a baby can hold.

Keep offering some larger strips alongside the small pieces — biting and tearing
are skills that need practising.

## Prep 12-17

However the table eats them, kept soft. Fillings become the thing to watch more
than the tortilla: salt, chilli and cheese add up quickly.

## Safety

**Whole corn kernels are their own entry on CDC's choking-hazard list — "cooked
or raw whole corn kernels" — and cooking does not clear it.** This is worth
saying precisely because most hazard rules on this app are discharged by cooking
something soft. This one is not. A kernel is a hazard cooked or raw.

**Only ground masa escapes that.** A tortilla, masa porridge, or anything where
the corn has been ground is a different food from a kernel. Corn on the cob,
canned corn, corn kernels stirred through rice or a stew, and corn kernels in a
tortilla filling are all the hazard as listed.

**Not crisp, not fried.** Tortilla chips, tostadas and hard taco shells are hard
food; CDC's principle is to cook hard foods until they are soft enough to mash
easily, and a chip is the opposite of that. That is a derived reading of a
general rule rather than a rule about chips, but it is not a close call.
