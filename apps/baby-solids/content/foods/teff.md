## Background

**Teff is the standout iron grain in this canon.** Cooked, it carries **2.05 mg
of iron per 100 g** — more than three times cooked [[quinoa]] and more than ten
times cooked [[millet]]. Uncooked it measures 7.63 mg per 100 g. For a whole
grain cooked at home, nothing else here comes close.

Two honest caveats sit next to that number.

**First, the iron may not all come from the grain.** Teff seeds are minute and
are traditionally threshed on the ground, and part of teff's measured iron is
attributed in the literature to soil contamination rather than to the plant
itself. **How much has not been quantified in anything this record could verify**,
so this record does not claim teff's iron is fully intrinsic — and does not
claim it isn't. What is measured is what USDA measured.

**Second, it is still non-heme iron**, so it absorbs far less efficiently than
the iron in [[beef]]. Serve a vitamin-C food in the same meal — [[potato]],
[[broccoli]], [[mango]] — which is CDC's own advice for non-heme sources.

Even at 2.05 mg, teff is not a substitute for [[fortified-infant-cereal]], which
is roughly thirty times higher per 100 g dry. It is, though, the best of the
home-cooked grains here by a wide margin.

## Prep 6-8

Teff cooks into a soft porridge in about fifteen minutes with plenty of water —
it is small enough to go creamy on its own. Cook it past that point rather than
short of it, then mash any remaining texture out and thin with breast milk,
formula or water.

**The grains are tiny, and that is not an exemption.** CDC's rule is to cook and
then finely grind or mash whole grain kernels; teff isn't named, "and other
grains" is, and cooked teff is a cooked whole kernel. In practice a well-cooked
teff porridge already meets this — you are checking, not doing extra work.

## Prep 9-11

Thicker porridge, and teff's mild molasses flavour carries other foods well —
mashed [[lentils]], [[black-beans]], puréed fruit. This is a good age to lean on
it, since iron requirements are highest and the grain is doing real work.

## Prep 12-17

However the household eats it, kept soft. If injera is on the table, read the
safety note below first — it is a different question from teff.

## Safety

**Injera is where the allergen risk is, and it is easy to miss.** Commercial
injera is frequently made from teff **blended with wheat**, and sometimes barley
or sorghum. **Wheat is one of the nine major US allergens**, so an injera made
this way is a wheat exposure — one that is invisible to anyone who has learned
"teff is gluten-free" and stopped there. Teff on its own contains no gluten.
Injera made from it may. **Read the label or ask, and do not treat injera as
gluten-free by default.**

**This record publishes no nutrition figures for injera at all.** USDA has no
record for it — zero entries — so any iron, calcium or protein number you see
attached to injera, including from us, would be made up. The teff figures above
are for the cooked grain.

Injera is usually eaten with berbere-spiced stews, which are salty and hot.
CDC's advice on sodium applies, and a spiced stew is not the way to introduce
either the bread or the grain. Serve it plain.

**Cooked grain kernels are on CDC's choking-hazard list**, and the instruction is
to cook then finely grind or mash. Teff porridge satisfies this by its nature;
teff cooked drier and served in grains does not.
