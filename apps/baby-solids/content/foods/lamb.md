## Background

Lamb is here for zinc as much as iron. Braised shoulder carries 2.39 mg of iron
and 6.08 mg of zinc per 100 g, and broiled shoulder 2.12 mg iron with 5.01 mg
zinc — the highest zinc values of any non-liver food in this set, and comfortably
above [[beef]]'s. Roasted leg is a little lower on both (1.98 mg iron, 4.66 mg
zinc), so if you are choosing a cut for a baby, shoulder wins twice: more of both
minerals, and it is the cut that braises into shreds.

The iron is heme iron, the form CDC says is more easily absorbed than the
non-heme iron in plants and fortified cereals. It does not need a vitamin-C food
alongside it — heme absorption is not vitamin-C dependent. Serve lamb with
whatever vegetable you like, for taste rather than chemistry.

Zinc matters at this age for the same reason iron does: WHO's 2023 complementary
feeding guideline asks for animal-source foods daily from six months, and the
Canadian Paediatric Society asks for iron-rich foods twice a day from about six
months. Lamb does both jobs in one ingredient.

Lamb is not one of the nine major US allergens.

## Prep 6-8

Take a shoulder cut, trim the fat, and remove any bone **before** cooking rather
than picking it out afterwards — that is CDC's instruction and it is much easier
in a raw piece than a hot cooked one. Braise it low and slow, in water or
unsalted stock, until it pulls apart with no resistance at all.

Then mince it finer than you would for yourself and stir in enough of the
cooking liquid that it clumps on a spoon. Dry mince scatters across a tray and
a six-month-old cannot gather it; wet mince holds together and gets eaten.

On temperature: USDA FSIS sets 145 °F plus a three-minute rest as the minimum
for lamb chops and roasts, and 160 °F for ground lamb. Those are floors, not
targets. A braise goes far past either, which is what you want here — FDA is
explicit that children should only eat meat cooked to a safe internal
temperature, so a pink or rare centre is not an option for a baby even though it
is legal for an adult.

## Prep 9-11

Shredded rather than minced. Once the pincer grasp arrives, long soft strands
are easier to pick up than fine crumbs, and lamb that has been braised properly
falls into exactly that shape. Mixed through [[oatmeal]] or a mashed vegetable it
travels better than lamb on its own.

## Prep 12-17

However the family eats it, with two things unchanged: still soft enough to mash,
and still not in chunks. Grilled lamb chops and kebab cubes are the wrong form —
firm cubes of meat stay a hazard well past the first birthday.

## Safety

**Chunks.** AAP keeps chunks of meat on its list of foods to avoid until age
four, and "tough or large chunks of meat" is a named entry on CDC's
choking-hazard list. Ground, finely minced, or shredded-to-collapse is the safe
form. A cube of lamb is not, at any age in this range.

**Bones.** Lamb shoulder and leg are usually sold bone-in. Bone out before
cooking — both CDC and NHS put bones on the remove-first list — then run the
cooked meat between your fingers before serving, because shoulder in particular
sheds small fragments during a long braise.

**Doneness.** FSIS's 145 °F plus rest is a food-safety minimum for a whole cut,
not a texture instruction. For a baby the texture is the harder test: if the
meat holds together when you pull at it, it is not ready, regardless of what the
thermometer says. Cook it further.
