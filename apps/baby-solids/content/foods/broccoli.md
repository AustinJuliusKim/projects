## Background

**Broccoli's job in a baby's diet is not its own iron — it is everybody else's.**
At 64.9 mg of vitamin C per 100 g it is one of the foods CDC names when it
advises pairing non-heme iron sources with vitamin C so more of the iron is
absorbed. Its own 0.67 mg of iron is a rounding error next to that.

So serve it *with* something: [[black-beans]], which contain no vitamin C at all,
or [[lentils]], which carry the most non-heme iron in this batch and only 1.5 mg
of C. Fortified [[oatmeal]] is the same case. Broccoli beside any of those is a
CDC-supported combination rather than a nice idea.

It is also the food NHS puts first by name. NHS recommends starting with the
less-sweet vegetables — "broccoli, cauliflower and spinach" — so that a baby
gets used to a range of flavours before the sweeter foods arrive.

Frozen broccoli is a legitimate substitute with one caveat worth knowing: USDA's
figures put frozen at 40.1 mg of vitamin C against fresh broccoli's 64.9 — about
a 38% drop. Iron is essentially unchanged at 0.61 mg. Frozen is still a good
vitamin-C food; fresh is a better one.

## Prep 6-8

Steam or boil florets until the thickest part of the stalk takes a knife with no
resistance — softer than you would serve yourself, softer than "tender-crisp".
Leave each floret attached to two or three centimetres of stalk so there is
something narrow to hold while the bushy head goes in the mouth.

The tree shape is why broccoli works early: the head is easy to gum and the stalk
is a built-in handle. Pieces cut small are harder at this age, not safer — a
baby who cannot pick them up ends up raking a handful in at once.

The length of that stalk is common practice, not a rule from anybody. AAP's
published dimensional guidance is the single instruction to cut food for infants
into pieces no larger than half an inch, and no body issues per-food shapes. What
carries the safety here is the cooking: if the stalk resists, it is not ready,
whatever length it is.

## Prep 9-11

Small soft pieces of head and stalk, picked up between finger and thumb. Keep a
whole floret on the tray too — biting a piece off is a separate skill from
picking one up, and it is worth practising.

## Prep 12-17

However the rest of the table is eating it, as long as it is cooked soft. Raw
broccoli and thick raw stalks stay out.

## Safety

Raw broccoli is a hard raw vegetable, the category CDC says usually needs cooking
so it mashes easily. Raw florets, crudités, and slaw are not a form to serve in
this age range.

The stalk is firmer than the head and is what to test — a head that mashes on a
stem that does not is the piece most likely to be swallowed in a lump. Press the
stalk, not the florets.
