## Background

Yogurt is not a food that squeaks past the cow's-milk rule. Before twelve months
it is the **preferred** form of cow's milk protein, and four bodies say so
independently: AAP calls plain, whole-fat or whole Greek yogurt "a good first
form of cow's milk protein" and puts dairy foods at around six months; CDC says
yogurt without added sugars can be introduced before twelve months; NHS puts it
from around six months; Health Canada from six to nine.

**Why the drink is prohibited and the food is not.** The rule against cow's milk
before twelve months is about *large-volume unmodified* milk. CDC names three
concrete problems with it: risk of intestinal bleeding, more protein and mineral
load than an immature kidney handles easily, and the wrong nutrient profile for
an infant. AAP adds that cow's-milk protein can irritate the lining of the
stomach and intestine, and frames the rule around **quantity**. The fourth
problem is displacement — a bottle of milk crowds out breast milk or formula and
the iron-rich food a baby needs at this age.

Yogurt escapes every one of those. It is eaten in food-sized portions rather
than drunk by the bottle — AAP's own 8-to-12-month sample menu puts it at 2 to 4
oz — so it does not displace the primary milk. And fermentation partially breaks
down both casein and lactose before the baby ever meets them. It is also the
usual vehicle for introducing the milk allergen early, which is why it is
recommended rather than merely tolerated.

**A real disagreement between national bodies, reported not resolved.** Health
Canada permits pasteurized homogenized cow milk as the *main* milk source from
**9 to 12 months** for an infant no longer breastfed. CDC and AAP say twelve
months, not before. This record does not pick a winner. Nothing about that
disagreement touches yogurt, which all four bodies allow from around six months.

**Iron is essentially zero** — 0.05 mg per 100 g — and this matters more than it
sounds. Dairy calcium *inhibits* absorption of plant (non-heme) iron. So do not
use yogurt as the "something alongside" for an iron meal: pair [[tofu]],
[[barley]] cereal, [[green-peas]] or [[spinach]] with a vitamin-C food like
[[mango]] instead, and serve the yogurt at a different sitting.

## Prep 6-8

Plain, pasteurized, whole-fat. Start with about a teaspoon, on its own or
stirred into something familiar.

Loading a spoon and handing it over handle-first is the practical move at this
age — a whole-hand grasp can hold a spoon long before it can fill one. A strip
of cooked [[butternut-squash]] or a finger of toast dipped in yogurt works as an
edible spoon and gives more grip.

Watch for about two hours after the first exposure. IgE reactions happen within
minutes to two hours, which is also why the familiar "wait 3–5 days before the
next new food" habit adds nothing diagnostically — it just slows down variety.

Greek yogurt is thicker and less likely to run off a spoon, which some babies
prefer and most trays do.

## Prep 9-11

Same food, more independence. This is a good age to let the baby hold the spoon
themselves and accept the mess; self-feeding a wet food is a skill with a
learning curve and no shortcut.

Stir in mashed fruit yourself rather than buying it pre-mixed — flavoured
yogurts marketed to babies carry added sugar, which is off the menu under 24
months.

## Prep 12-17

However the household eats it. Plain yogurt with fruit stirred in, as a dip, in
a smoothie, spooned over grains.

Keep it whole-fat. Babies should get roughly half their calories from fat, and
AAP does not recommend restricting fat before age two.

## Safety

Three hard rules, all of them about the pot you buy rather than how you serve it.

**Plain only.** AAP is explicit about avoiding the added sugar commonly found in
yogurt marketed to babies. No added sugars under 24 months.

**Pasteurized only.** This applies to the yogurt and to any milk used to make
it.

**Whole-fat, not low-fat.** Reduced-fat dairy is an adult recommendation applied
to the wrong body.

Yogurt itself is not a choking risk — it is already a safe consistency. The
thing to check is what has been mixed *into* it: whole grapes, whole berries,
chunks of dried fruit and granola clusters in an adult pot are all on the hazard
lists in their own right.

Yogurt is not a substitute for breast milk or formula. It is a food that sits
alongside them, not a drink that replaces them.
