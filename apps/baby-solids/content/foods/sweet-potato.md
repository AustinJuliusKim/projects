## Background

A soft, sweet root vegetable that holds its shape when roasted, which makes it
one of the easier first foods to hand over whole. Rich in beta-carotene, and
its vitamin C helps the body absorb non-heme iron from the same meal — worth
pairing with beans, lentils, or fortified cereal.

Related: [[kabocha]] behaves almost identically once cooked.

## Prep 6-8

Roast or steam until a fork slides in with no resistance, then cut into
finger-length wedges about as thick as two of your fingers. Leave the skin on
for a beginner — it gives something to grip, and the flesh is what gets eaten.

At this age the palmar grasp means the whole piece is held in the fist with an
end sticking out, so the baby controls the bite. Pieces cut small are actually
*harder* now, not safer: they can't be picked up, and they end up scooped by
the handful.

## Prep 9-11

Once the pincer grasp appears — usually around nine months — switch to soft
cubes roughly the size of your thumbnail. Keep offering a few larger pieces
alongside, since biting and tearing are their own skills worth practising.

## Prep 12-17

Whatever the rest of the table is having — cubed, mashed, or in wedges. Keep
offering some pieces big enough to bite from rather than only pincer-sized
ones; biting and tearing are still skills in progress.

## Safety

Cooked sweet potato is low risk once it mashes with light pressure between two
fingers. Undercooked pieces are firm enough to be a hazard, so test a piece
before serving rather than trusting the timer.

Raw sweet potato is a hard raw vegetable and is not appropriate at any age in
this range.
