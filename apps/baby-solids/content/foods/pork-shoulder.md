## Background

Pork shoulder is the weakest red meat in this set for iron: 1.32 mg per 100 g
roasted, or 1.50 mg trimmed to lean only. That is under half of [[beef]]'s
2.93 mg and well below [[lamb]]'s. It still carries 3.71 mg of zinc, which is
respectable, and the iron it does have is heme iron — the readily absorbed form
CDC contrasts with the non-heme iron in plants and fortified cereals.

So think of pork shoulder as a good food that is not your iron workhorse. If
your week's iron is coming from one animal food, beef or lamb does more of the
job. Pork earns its place on texture and price: shoulder is cheap, forgiving,
and slow-cooks into shreds more reliably than almost anything else, which makes
it easy to serve to a baby who is still learning to gather food.

Pork is not one of the nine major US allergens.

## Prep 6-8

Trim the fat cap and remove skin and bone **before** cooking — CDC is explicit
about that order, and it means nothing has to be picked out of the finished
meat. Braise or slow-roast the shoulder in water or unsalted stock until it
falls apart under a fork with no resistance.

Mince it fine, then stir in enough of the unsalted cooking liquid or a familiar
puree that it clumps rather than scatters. Pork dries out faster than beef as it
cools, so add more liquid than feels necessary and check it again just before
serving.

USDA FSIS sets 145 °F plus a three-minute rest as the minimum for fresh pork
roasts and chops, and 160 °F for ground pork. A long braise passes both by a
wide margin, which is the point: for a baby you are cooking for texture, and the
safe temperature arrives long before the meat is soft enough.

## Prep 9-11

Shredded. Slow-cooked shoulder pulls into exactly the long soft strands a pincer
grasp handles well, and strands are easier to pick up than fine mince. Stirred
through mashed vegetables or [[oatmeal]] it stays moist longer than it does on a
bare tray.

## Prep 12-17

However the rest of the table is eating it, as long as it is fresh pork, still
soft, and still cut small rather than cubed. Large chunks of meat do not drop off
CDC's hazard list at twelve months.

## Safety

**Fresh pork only.** This is the instruction that matters most here. Bacon, ham,
pancetta, prosciutto, chorizo, salami, sausage, spam, and 햄 are excluded for two
separate reasons, either of which would be enough on its own:

- **Sodium.** They are salt-cured or salt-seasoned products, and CDC advises
  against high-sodium foods for children under two. We publish no sodium figure
  for any of them — none was verified against a primary source while this record
  was written, and an unverified number on a page about a baby's food is worse
  than no number.
- **Choking.** AAP names meat sticks and sausages directly on its list of foods
  to avoid, and CDC's shape rule for cylindrical foods is to cut them into short
  thin strips rather than coins. A sausage is the exact hazard those rules
  describe.

"Cooked pork" on a label is not the same as fresh pork. If it was cured, smoked,
or brined before it reached you, it belongs to the paragraph above.

**Chunks.** Fresh pork still has to be ground, minced, or shredded. Cubes of
meat are a named choking-hazard entry, and pork cubes in particular tend to stay
springy rather than mashing.

**Doneness.** Use a thermometer for the food-safety floor and your fingers for
the texture. If a shred holds its shape when you pull at it, keep cooking.
