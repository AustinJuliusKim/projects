## Background

AAP names "well-cooked, finely chopped chicken" as a finger food, and the
Canadian Paediatric Society lists cooked chicken among iron-rich first foods.
That is unusually specific for a guideline body — most of them describe
textures, not dishes — so the form to serve is not a matter of interpretation
here.

The iron is heme iron, the readily absorbed kind, at 1.13 mg per 100 g roasted.
That is well below [[beef]]'s, so if the week's iron is coming from one animal
food, beef does more of the work. Chicken's advantage is that it is cheap,
mild, and goes with everything.

Thigh rather than breast is a texture preference, not a guideline position: dark
meat stays moist through longer cooking, and dry chicken breast is the most
common way a promising meat meal ends up on the floor. Breast is not unsafe —
it just needs more help staying wet.

## Prep 6-8

Take the skin, fat, and bones off **before** cooking, not after — CDC is
explicit about the order, and it means no bone fragments end up in the finished
meat. Braise, poach, or roast the thigh until it pulls apart with a fork, cook
all poultry to 165 °F (USDA FSIS), then chop it finely and stir in enough
unsalted broth or pan juices that it holds together on a spoon.

Run the chopped meat between your fingers before serving. Thigh meat carries
small tendons and the occasional splinter of bone, and that check takes ten
seconds.

## Prep 9-11

Shredded rather than chopped — long soft strands are easy to pick up with a
pincer grasp and easy to gum apart. Chicken mixed into [[oatmeal]] or mashed
vegetables still travels better than chicken alone.

## Prep 12-17

However the family eats it, still soft and still cut small. "Tough or large
chunks of meat" does not drop off CDC's hazard list at twelve months, and neither
do bones.

## Safety

Bones and chunks are the hazard, and CDC handles both with one instruction:
remove all fat, skin, and bones from poultry before cooking, then cook until it
mashes easily with a fork.

Cook all poultry to 165 °F. Undercooked chicken is a food-safety problem, not
just an unpleasant texture, and an infant is the worst person to test it on — no
pink at the bone, and a thermometer rather than "it looked done".
