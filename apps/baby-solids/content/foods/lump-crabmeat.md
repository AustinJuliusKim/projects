## Background

Crab closes the same allergen slot as [[shrimp]] — **crustacean shellfish**, one
of the nine major US allergens — and it is worth knowing that either one does
that job, so you do not need both.

**The shell fragment is the hazard, not the meat.** This is the whole reason
crab gets its own record. Crabmeat itself is soft, flaky and about as low-risk a
texture as exists. But pasteurized lump crab, the tub sold in the refrigerated
case, routinely carries shell and cartilage shards that survived picking at the
plant. They are thin, pale, sharp-edged and near-invisible against white meat.
The preparation step that matters is not cutting. It is picking over.

**This record publishes no iron figure and no USDA id.** A verified composition
entry for lump crabmeat could not be established while this was written, and the
honest options were to leave it blank or to borrow [[shrimp]]'s numbers and call
them crab's. It is left blank. Do not read that as "crab has no iron" — read it
as "nobody here has checked." Crab is a protein food in this canon; if you need
an iron food, use [[sardines]], [[beef]] or [[lentils]], which have figures
behind them.

Sodium is the other gap. Crab is typically packed with salt and the actual
figure is unverified, so rinse it, do not add more, and do not treat it as a
low-sodium protein.

**On "shellfish" as a category:** FDA splits it. Crustacean — crab, shrimp,
lobster, crawfish — is a major allergen and must be declared on labels.
**Molluscs — oyster, clam, mussel, scallop — are not**, and carry no mandatory
declaration, even though a mollusc allergy is real and can be severe. Everyday
usage merges the two. Regulation does not.

## Prep 6-8

Use cooked, refrigerated lump crabmeat, or meat you picked yourself from a crab
you cooked. Tip it into a shallow white bowl or plate, spread it out, and go
through it with your fingertips under a bright light — the whole tub, not a
spot check. You are feeling for anything firmer than the meat: a translucent
sliver, a curved chip, a papery membrane. It is slow, and it is the job.

Then flake it fine and stir it into something moist — mashed [[avocado]],
[[sweet-potato]], a thick puree — so it holds together instead of scattering
into dry threads.

Serve it fully cooked. NHS's rule for shellfish is never raw or lightly cooked.

## Prep 9-11

Larger flakes the pincer grasp can gather, still picked over the same way, still
mixed through something that binds it. Nothing about the shell check relaxes
with age; a nine-month-old is not better at spitting out a shell chip than a
seven-month-old.

## Prep 12-17

Crab folded into whatever the family eats — a rice bowl, a soft cake, a soup.
Still picked over. Still fully cooked. Still no imitation crab.

## Safety

**Pick it over by hand, under good light, every time.** Not once per tub, every
serving. Shell and cartilage shards are the entire risk profile of this food,
and no amount of cooking softens them. Crab is not a named entry on any official
choking list — this instruction is an application of CDC's general principle of
removing bones before the food reaches a child, and it is labelled as such.

**Never imitation crab.** Surimi sticks are not a substitute here and swapping
them in is actively counterproductive on three counts: they are fish paste plus
starch, so they add **wheat** in most formulations, they carry added salt, and
they **do not close the crustacean slot** — a baby who tolerates imitation crab
has been introduced to fish and wheat, not to crab. If you are serving crab to
cover the allergen, the label has to say crab.

**Never raw or lightly cooked**, per NHS.

**Do not read a tolerated crab as a tolerated scallop.** Crustacean and mollusc
are separate allergens, and tolerating one says nothing about the other.
