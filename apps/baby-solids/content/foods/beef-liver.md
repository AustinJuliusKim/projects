## Background

Beef liver is the densest iron and zinc in ordinary food: 6.54 mg of iron and
5.30 mg of zinc per 100 g braised, which is more than twice [[beef]] mince on
iron. It is heme iron, the readily absorbed form.

**And iron is not the constraint on this food. Vitamin A is.**

Braised beef liver carries 9,440 µg RAE of preformed vitamin A per 100 g. NIH
ODS sets the Tolerable Upper Intake Level for preformed vitamin A at **600 µg
per day from birth to 12 months**, and says chronic intake above the UL can
cause congenital birth defects. Those two numbers are both issued, both
verified, and they are three orders of magnitude apart per unit weight. That
gap, not the iron, is what governs how this food is served.

If you want the arithmetic: dividing the UL by the density puts 600 µg at
roughly **6 g of braised liver**, or about 8 g pan-fried. **That is arithmetic
we did, not guidance anyone issued** — no body has published a gram figure for
infant liver, and you should treat ours as an illustration of the scale rather
than a portion to measure out. What it illustrates is that a normal-looking
spoonful of liver is already past the daily ceiling.

**Where the guidance actually stands.** No health body names liver as an infant
food, and none prohibits it either. That is an absence of guidance, not
permission and not a warning — we are reporting it rather than filling it in.
The one number that is issued and applies to a baby is the 600 µg/day UL. The
once-a-week limit on this page is convention, and the frequency note explains
exactly whose convention it is.

[[chicken-liver]] carries roughly half the vitamin A density and about twice the
iron, which makes it the better-value liver on both axes if you are choosing
one.

## Prep 6-8

Rinse the liver, pull off the thin outer membrane, and cut out the pale tubes —
all before cooking. Braise or gently pan-cook it until it is firm and uniformly
brown through the middle, with no red or bloody centre.

**There is no cooking temperature on this page, and that is deliberate.** USDA
FSIS issues no safe internal temperature for beef liver: it is not on the safe
temperature chart, whose beef row covers steaks, chops and roasts, and it is not
in FSIS's beef handling guidance. Rather than borrow a number from a different
cut, we describe the doneness test instead — cooked through, firm, no red in the
centre. FDA is explicit that children should only eat meat cooked to a safe
internal temperature, so undercooked liver is out even though the number is
missing. (Chicken liver *does* have an issued temperature, because FSIS covers
it as a giblet. Beef liver does not.)

Then puree or mince it very fine and stir a **small** amount into a food your
baby already eats — a mashed vegetable, [[oatmeal]], or a mince the baby is used
to. Liver on its own is dry, grainy and strong-tasting, and a baby will usually
refuse it; blended in, it disappears.

## Prep 9-11

The same form and the same small amount. Nothing about the pincer grasp changes
the vitamin A arithmetic, so this does not become a finger food or a portion in
its own right — it stays an ingredient stirred through something else.

## Prep 12-17

Still blended in, still bounded by the frequency note. One caution about the
numbers as your baby gets older: the 600 µg/day UL we cite is the figure for
**birth to 12 months**. We have not verified a figure for the year after that,
so this record does not print one, and the honest thing is to keep the same
caution rather than assume the ceiling rises.

Liver pâté, parfait, and 간 요리 built on cured or heavily salted bases stay out —
those are salt products as much as liver products.

## Safety

**Vitamin A is the hazard, and it is cumulative.** Preformed vitamin A — the
animal form, which is what liver contains — is stored rather than excreted, so
the risk is not from one meal but from repetition. That is why this record
carries a frequency limit at all, and why it is the only food in the canon that
needs one.

Read the frequency limit for exactly what it says: the 600 µg/day UL is issued
by NIH ODS and applies to your baby. The once-a-week ceiling is not issued by
anyone for a baby. It comes from an NHS page written for adults that does not
mention children, and applying an adult rule to a body a fraction of the weight
is an extrapolation. We have made that extrapolation in the cautious direction
and labelled it, because the alternative was to publish no limit at all beside a
food carrying 15 times the daily UL in a 100 g portion. If you want a number for
your own baby, this is a good question for your pediatrician — and one they can
actually answer, because it depends on what else your baby eats.

**No cooking temperature exists.** See the prep note. Cook it through and judge
by colour and firmness, and do not let anyone tell you liver is best served
pink — for a baby it is not.

**Texture.** Cooked liver goes grainy and can pack at the back of the mouth. It
needs to be pureed or minced very fine and carried in something moist, which is
also how it becomes edible.

**Supplements.** If your baby is on a vitamin A-containing supplement or a
multivitamin, that counts toward the same 600 µg ceiling. Check the label before
adding liver to the week.
