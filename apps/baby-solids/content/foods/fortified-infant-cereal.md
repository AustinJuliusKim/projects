## Background

**This is the single largest nutritional lever in the whole canon, and it is one
of the very few places where a guideline body names specific foods.** CDC's
wording is to "offer a variety of fortified infant cereals such as oat, barley,
and multi-grain instead of only rice cereal," and FDA names the same three in
its arsenic guidance. Most infant-feeding advice is deliberately generic; this
is not.

**The numbers, per 100 g dry.** Fortified infant **oat** carries **64.1 mg of
iron** and 12.7 mg of zinc. Fortified infant **barley**: 48.2 mg of iron, 3.13
mg of zinc. Fortified infant **multigrain**: 30.0 mg of iron, 10.7 mg of zinc.
For comparison, plain unfortified oats are 4.25 mg of iron dry — and **0.90 mg
once cooked in water.**

Two things fall out of those figures.

**Fortified oat carries about seventy-one times the iron of the porridge you
cook from a canister of oats.** Both get called "oatmeal" in a kitchen. They are
not the same food nutritionally, and if the homemade pot quietly replaces the
box, almost all of the iron leaves the diet without anything looking different.

**Fortified oat also carries more than double fortified multigrain** — 64.1
against 30.0. If iron is the goal, single-grain fortified oat is the strongest
non-rice option in USDA's data.

**Non-rice, specifically.** Rice cereal is fortified too, but rice concentrates
inorganic arsenic and CDC's concern is a rice-only diet: "only feeding your
child infant rice cereal can increase their risk of arsenic exposure." See
[[short-grain-rice]] for the detail. Rotating oat, barley and multigrain costs
nothing and removes the question.

**"Fortified" is not one thing — read the panel.** One organic rice cereal in
USDA's data is labelled *fortified only with iron*: 47.5 mg of iron and **1.84
mg of zinc**, roughly a sixfold zinc shortfall against the other fortified
cereals in the table. Zinc matters at this age for the same reason iron does.
The word on the front of the box does not tell you which nutrients are in it;
the panel on the back does.

Added iron is non-heme and absorbs inefficiently, so serve a vitamin-C food in
the same meal — [[mango]], [[broccoli]], [[potato]] — and keep dairy out of that
particular bowl, since calcium inhibits non-heme absorption. See [[oatmeal]] and
[[barley]] for the single-grain records.

## Prep 6-8

Put a spoonful of dry cereal in a bowl and mix with breast milk, formula, or
water until it is smooth and just thick enough to stay on a loaded spoon. Start
thinner than you think and thicken it over the following weeks — moving food
from the front of the mouth to the back is a skill, and the thickening is how it
gets practised.

Hand over the loaded spoon, or hold it and let the baby take it off. Most of the
first fortnight lands on the tray and the chin. That is the job at this age.

**The dry-weight figures above are for the powder, not the bowl.** What actually
reaches your baby depends on how thickly you mix it. The comparison between
cereals still holds.

## Prep 9-11

Thicker and lumpier, and increasingly self-fed. This is the age the cereal earns
its keep as a carrier: mashed [[black-beans]], flaked [[salmon]], puréed
[[spinach]] or fruit all stir in and go down easily, which gets the iron and the
vitamin C into one bowl.

Iron requirements are at their highest across this band, so if fortified cereal
is going to be in the diet at all, this is when it counts.

## Prep 12-17

Still useful, still the easiest iron in the house, and by now one component of a
varied diet rather than the centre of it. If you have moved on to whole grains
cooked at home, keep CDC's kernel rule in view — cook and then finely grind or
mash whole-grain kernels — and keep an iron food somewhere in the day.

## Safety

**Mix with breast milk, formula, or water. Not cow's milk before 12 months.**

Smooth cereal is a low-risk texture; that is most of why it exists. The hazard
appears when you switch to home-cooked whole grains, where **cooked whole grain
kernels are on CDC's choking-hazard list** and the instruction is to cook and
then finely grind or mash them.

**On allergens:** oat and barley are not among the nine major US allergens.
Multigrain cereals often contain wheat, which **is** one of the nine — so if
wheat matters in your household, the ingredient list is what settles it, not the
word "multigrain."
