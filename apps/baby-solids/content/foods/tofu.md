## Background

Tofu has the strongest first-food credentials of anything in this batch: Health
Canada, CDC, AAP and NHS all name it, and Health Canada lists it among iron-rich
*first* foods — the category it treats as the priority at six months. It is
soft, compressible, appears on no choking-hazard list, and needs almost no
preparation.

**The iron depends entirely on how the tofu was set, and the spread is about
fivefold.** Tofu is made by curdling soy milk with a coagulant, and the
coagulant ends up in the food. Per 100 g, USDA's verified entries run: regular
tofu set with calcium sulfate, **5.36 mg** iron and 350 mg calcium; firm
calcium-sulfate tofu, 2.66 mg iron and 683 mg calcium; hard tofu set with nigari
(magnesium chloride), 2.75 mg and 345 mg; silken firm tofu, 1.03 mg and 32 mg;
and dried-frozen koyadofu, 9.73 mg and 364 mg.

Silken tofu carries a fifth to a tenth of the iron of calcium-set regular tofu.
If you are buying tofu *for* the iron — and at 5.36 mg per 100 g against an
11 mg daily target for a 7–12 month old, it is one of the few plant foods that
genuinely moves that number — read the ingredients panel for calcium sulfate.
The figure in this record is that form.

**The honest complication:** the same calcium that makes calcium-sulfate tofu
iron-rich also inhibits absorption of plant (non-heme) iron. You cannot resolve
that from the label, and no guideline body addresses it. What you can do is the
thing that helps regardless — serve a vitamin-C food in the same meal, since
vitamin C improves non-heme absorption. [[mango]], [[green-peas]] or
[[butternut-squash]] all work. Do not use [[yogurt]] as that partner: dairy
calcium works against you here.

**On soy and hormones.** Claims about phytoestrogens in infant diets are not
supported by any AAP, CDC or Health Canada prohibition, and all four of those
bodies name tofu as a food to give babies. This record does not repeat them as
guidance.

## Prep 6-8

Buy firm or extra-firm. Drain it, wrap it in a clean towel, and press it under
something heavy for ten minutes — it firms up and stops falling apart in a fist.

Cut finger-length batons about as thick as two of your fingers. The whole-hand
grasp means the baton is held in a fist with an end sticking out and the baby
sets their own bite size. Plain and cold is fine; pan-frying briefly gives a
slightly firmer surface that holds together better, with no oil needed beyond a
film.

Tofu is slippery. Rolling a baton in ground oats, infant cereal, or a little
ground sesame gives grip — that is a handling aid, not a safety measure.

Mashed tofu also mixes into anything: purées, [[barley]] cereal, mashed
vegetables.

Serve it plain. Soy sauce is very high in sodium and is not appropriate before
24 months.

## Prep 9-11

With the pincer grasp — most often from 9 to 10 months per AAP — cubes about the
size of your thumbnail are easy to pick up, and tofu holds a cube shape better
than most soft foods. Keep a couple of larger batons alongside so biting and
tearing stay in practice.

Crumbled firm tofu scrambled in a pan takes on other flavours well and is an
easy way to carry a vegetable the baby is less sure about.

## Prep 12-17

However the household eats it — in soup, stir-fried, crumbled through
vegetables, in a soft stew. Silken tofu comes into its own here as a texture
rather than an iron source.

Watch the seasoning rather than the tofu: soy sauce, doenjang and most stir-fry
sauces carry more sodium than a toddler portion should, so take the child's
share out before the sauce goes in.

## Safety

There is very little to flag, which is unusual on this list. Tofu is soft,
compresses under pressure, and sits on no AAP or CDC hazard list. Cut it to a
size the baby can hold and it needs nothing else.

Two ordinary handling points. Keep opened tofu refrigerated and covered in
water, and use it within a few days — it is a fresh, high-protein food and it
spoils. And check that what you bought is plain: flavoured, smoked and marinated
tofu is usually salted heavily.
