## Background

Cassava — yuca, manioc — is a staple starch across West Africa, Latin America
and Southeast Asia, and a properly prepared one is a perfectly reasonable food
for a baby. It comes with a preparation rule that is not optional, and one
number worth knowing before you build meals around it.

**It carries the lowest iron of any food in this part of the canon: 0.27 mg per
100 g.** That is a starch, not a nutritional contribution. Cassava fills a baby
up, which is its own risk at an age when appetite is limited and iron
requirements are at their lifetime peak. Serve it alongside iron — [[beef]],
[[lentils]], [[black-beans]], [[fortified-infant-cereal]] — rather than as the
centre of a meal.

**Raw cassava contains cyanogenic glycosides** — linamarin and lotaustralin —
which release cyanide when the plant tissue is damaged. Concentration "varies
greatly between varieties," so you cannot tell by looking, and the preparation
below assumes you are working with a root that has some. That is the safe
assumption and it costs you nothing but time.

This record publishes **no cyanogen concentration for retail cassava**, because
no verified figure for what is on a US or Korean shelf exists in the sources
behind this canon. Numbers circulate; none of them survived checking.

## Prep 6-8

**Peel it completely.** FAO is direct that "the cyanide content is substantially
higher in the cassava peel," so the peel is not a marginal loss — it is the part
you are removing on purpose. Take the woody core out too.

**Cut it up, put it in cold water, and bring it to the boil from cold.** This is
the one preparation detail that changes the outcome measurably: after thirty
minutes of cooking, residual cyanide is **about 8 percent of the starting level
when the cassava went in cold**, against **about 30 percent when it was dropped
into already-boiling water** — roughly a fourfold difference for a step that
takes no extra effort. Soaking the peeled pieces for 18 to 24 hours beforehand
cuts levels by around half again, and is worth doing if you have the time.

Boil until a piece falls apart under a fork with no resistance anywhere, then
mash and thin with the cooking liquid, breast milk or formula.

## Prep 9-11

Same preparation, mashed rather than puréed, mixed with something carrying iron
and something carrying vitamin C. Cassava is bland and heavy, so it works best
as a base under a bean or meat mash rather than as a bowl of its own.

## Prep 12-17

However the household eats boiled or stewed cassava, as long as it has been
peeled and cooked right through. Take the baby's portion out before salt.

Fried yuca is hard-edged and salted, which is the wrong direction on both
counts.

## Safety

**Never raw. Never lightly cooked. Never grated raw into anything, and never in
a smoothie.** FAO records the symptoms of eating raw or insufficiently processed
cassava as "vertigo, vomiting, collapse and in some cases death." That is the
reason the prep instructions above have no shortcuts in them, and it is the
whole of why this food is rated modify-required rather than low.

**Peel completely and boil through from cold water.** Both halves matter: the
peel is where the cyanogens concentrate, and starting cold gives roughly four
times the reduction of dropping the pieces into boiling water.

**Cassava flour, tapioca, tapioca pearls and garri are separately processed
foods, and none of this transfers to them in either direction.** They are made
by grating, pressing, fermenting, drying or roasting, and each process handles
the cyanogens differently. Do not read this record as clearing them, and do not
read a packet of tapioca starch as evidence that the root is easy. Tapioca
pearls in particular are a distinct choking hazard and are not addressed here.

**Vitamin C:** raw cassava measures 20.6 mg per 100 g, but the long boil this
food requires destroys much of it, and no verified figure exists for the cooked
root. So this record does not present cassava as a vitamin-C partner for an iron
meal. Use [[potato]], [[broccoli]] or [[mango]] for that.
