## Background

A sweet winter squash that roasts to a texture a six-month-old can manage
without any special technique. NHS names squash among the vegetables to start
weaning with, and CDC names squash at the category level.

Treat it as a negligible iron source. USDA's two datasets disagree by roughly
threefold — the legacy cooked entry gives 0.6 mg of iron and 15.1 mg of vitamin
C per 100 g, while the newer Foundation sample gives 0.213 mg and 7.6 mg. The
figure in this record is the higher of the two, and even that is small next to
the 11 mg a day a 7–12 month old needs. Its real job at the table is as a
vitamin-C partner: serve it alongside an iron food such as [[tofu]],
[[green-peas]], or [[barley]] cereal, since vitamin C improves absorption of
plant (non-heme) iron eaten in the same meal.

[[kabocha]] behaves almost identically once cooked, and the prep below applies
to both.

## Prep 6-8

Halve it, scoop the seeds, and roast cut-side down at around 200 °C / 400 °F
until a fork slides in with no resistance — usually 35 to 45 minutes. Steaming
works too but leaves it wetter and harder to hold.

Cut finger-length wedges about as thick as two of your fingers. At this age the
whole-hand grasp means the piece is held in a fist with an end sticking out, so
the baby controls the bite. Small pieces are harder now, not safer — they can't
be picked up and end up scooped by the handful.

Skin on or off is your call. Roasted butternut skin is edible and gives
something to grip; peeled is tidier and NHS notes that removing skin makes food
easier to swallow.

## Prep 9-11

Once the pincer grasp appears — AAP puts that at most often between 9 and 10
months — switch to soft cubes about the size of your thumbnail, keeping a few
larger wedges alongside so biting and tearing stay in practice.

Mashed butternut is also a good carrier for something the baby is less sure
about: iron-rich purées, a spoonful of yogurt, ground meat.

## Prep 12-17

However the rest of the table is eating it — cubed, mashed, in soup, roasted in
wedges. Keep some pieces big enough to bite from rather than only pincer-sized
ones.

## Safety

Undercooked squash is firm enough to be a hazard. Test a piece between your own
fingers rather than trusting the timer; if it holds its shape under pressure, it
needs longer.

Home-made vegetable purées, squash included, should be cooled quickly,
refrigerated promptly, and not kept for days.

That advice used to have a formal citation behind it: AAP's 2005 clinical report
on infant methemoglobinemia named well water as the main risk vector, found
little or no risk from commercially prepared US infant food, and noted that
cases from home-prepared vegetable foods kept too long continued to occur.
**AAP retired that report in June 2025**, so it is no longer current guidance
and nothing has replaced it. The storage habit stands on its own merits — cool
it fast, refrigerate it, don't keep it for days — but it is now ordinary food
hygiene rather than a cited recommendation. [[spinach]] covers the whole
argument, including where US and EU advice still differ.
